The Ball-Security Mirage: Winnipeg's Offence Hasn't Actually Improved

November 15, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Arithmetic Behind a 4.4-Point Jump

The number Mike O'Shea keeps getting asked about is 25.7. Winnipeg's offensive points per game in 2026, up from 21.3 a year earlier. On its face, that's a coordinator hire working. On closer inspection, it's one of the more misleading year-over-year deltas in the CFL.

Start with volume. The Bombers generated 2.7 more net yards per game than in 2025, and 0.3 more yards per play. Those are rounding errors. The offence moved the ball essentially the same distance it moved it a year ago. What actually changed was possession security: 34 giveaways, down from 45. Eleven fewer possessions gifted to the opposition, in a league where a short field is worth roughly four to five points once you price in drive-start field position. Run that math and the scoring jump is nearly fully explained by ball security and the field position it bought, plus a special-teams unit that ranked first league-wide and kept handing Collaros the ball on the good side of the fifty.

Here's the awkward part for anyone arguing the offence genuinely levelled up: by percentage, Winnipeg's 20.7 percent scoring climb actually outpaced the league's 8.7 percent rise. But the Bombers still finished seventh in points scored, seventh in net yardage and seventh in yards per play. That combination tells you the 2025 floor was historically low relative to peers, and that the six offences ahead of them improved at least as much. Real improvement from a very bad base is still a seventh-place offence.

42 Sacks, Six-Man Protection, and a 38-Year-Old Quarterback

The clearest film-level indictment is the sack number: 42 allowed, five more than 2025, on a team that threw the ball no more often. That's a protection problem, and it isn't a one-position problem.

Winnipeg's answers against pressure were structural rather than schematic. Condell leaned on six-man protections with Brady Oliveira scanning the A-gap, which keeps the back in rather than releasing him into the flat as a hot answer. The result: max protection with only three or four routes, and Zach Collaros holding the ball an extra beat because the read tree was slow-developing. Against Edmonton and Saskatchewan fronts that run twist games and delayed second-level blitzes, that's exactly the look that produces free runners up the middle. Collaros can still diagnose it. At 38, he can't consistently escape it.

The counter-argument is that Condell's Hamilton tape was built on rhythm, three-step, quick game, rub concepts to beat press, and a heavy dose of play-action off inside zone. Collaros thrived in that system a decade ago. Winnipeg's 2026 version drifted away from it in exactly the games that mattered, and the five-sack regression is the receipt.

Seventh in Yards Per Play Is a Tendency Problem

The 0.3-yard gain per snap is where play-calling tendency becomes visible. Winnipeg's early-down profile stayed conservative: first-down runs into loaded boxes to "establish" a physical identity that the 2026 roster no longer supports at the point of attack. That sets up second-and-eight, and second-and-long is where Condell's call sheet got narrower, verticals out of empty, or a checkdown to the back that conceded the down.

The receiver room amplified it. Nic Demski remains the chain-mover, but the Bombers lacked a consistent second-level separator who could win on second-and-medium against off-coverage. When the only reliable answer is a contested catch to a Covered receiver, an offence grades out seventh in efficiency no matter who's calling it. O'Shea's Tuesday line, "Because it's three different coordinators, I think you've gotta look at it differently", was read as a defence of Condell. It was closer to an admission that the problem sits above the coordinator's desk, in roster construction and offensive line investment.

The Defence Is Holding a Window Open

The reason any of this matters is that Winnipeg's other two phases are carrying the team. The defence tied for fifth league-wide; special teams ranked first. That's a contending infrastructure attached to a bottom-tier offence, and it is why the season ended in Montreal rather than in a rebuild.

The East Final tape is the whole thesis in one game. A fifth-ranked defence kept Winnipeg in it; a seventh-ranked offence couldn't close it; and the margin showed up as a discipline breakdown. Davis Alexander's post-game confrontation with O'Shea, "I respect the hell out of you, but 45 was talking about my dad", centred on David Reese's alleged taunting about Alexander's late father, Matt, who died of lung cancer in 2024. O'Shea's response was the most instructive thing said all week: "The toughest guys on the field never really have to speak.. I certainly don't want to take penalties because of it." He's describing a team that loses the emotional ledger in tight second halves. That's a coaching-adjacent cost, and it's a real one.

Condell is under contract through 2027 and O'Shea expects him back. Fine. But the 4.4-point mirage won't repeat itself, turnover margins of that size regress, and 34 giveaways is not a sustainable floor. If Winnipeg's offence is going to actually close the gap on the six teams ahead of it, it needs to come from protection design, first-down aggression, and a second receiver who can beat off-coverage. Not from ball security. Not again.
