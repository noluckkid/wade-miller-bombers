The Bombers Ranked Seventh in Everything That Matters. Now What?

November 18, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

One Rank, Three Playbooks

Start with the number that should keep the football operations staff up at night: seventh. Winnipeg finished the 2026 season ranked seventh league-wide in points scored, seventh in net yardage, and seventh in yards per play. Not seventh in one category and fourth in another. Seventh in all three, in lockstep, across a nine-team league. That is not a scheme problem. That is a systemic ceiling, and it survived a coordinator change.

The Bombers have now cycled through three offensive coordinators in three years. Buck Pierce in 2024, Jason Hogan in 2025, Tommy Condell in 2026, and the output has barely budged. O'Shea, speaking Tuesday, pushed back on the comparison: "Because it's three different coordinators, I think you've gotta look at it differently. I don't think I'd be looking at this year and comparing it to last year and comparing it to the year before." Respectfully, that's backwards. Three coordinators producing three functionally identical rankings is the strongest possible argument that the coordinator is not the variable. The constants are the quarterback, the protection, and the receiving corps, and O'Shea's own numbers indict all three.

Yes, the raw totals moved: 25.7 offensive points per game, up from 21.3; 2.7 more yards per game; 0.3 more yards per play; 34 giveaways instead of 45. Real improvement, in a vacuum. But league-wide scoring jumped 8.7 percent, which means a 4.4-point-per-game bump bought Winnipeg exactly nothing in relative terms. They ran to stay in place. The turnover reduction is the one genuine, scheme-independent win, protecting the ball is a quarterback decision, and Collaros made better ones.

Protection Isn't a Coordinator Problem

The number that explains the seventh-place finish is 42, sacks allowed, five more than 2025, on a team that threw fewer explosive plays than almost anyone. A 0.3-yard-per-play improvement paired with a five-sack regression tells you where the pressure came from: the pocket collapsing inward, not the play caller getting cute.

Watch the film on Collaros and the through-line across all three coordinators is identical. Pierce built off wide-zone play action and vertical shots. Hogan spread the field. Condell, who has coached Collaros before, leans on timing and anticipation, intermediate concepts thrown before the break. All three systems share one prerequisite: a clean interior and a quarterback who can step into a throw on rhythm. Collaros has never been a scrambler. When the A-gaps get walked back into his lap, the entire timing tree dies at the top of the drop, and every route concept on the call sheet becomes a checkdown. Seventh in yards per play is what that looks like on paper.

Which makes Tuesday's eight-player signing batch worth reading closely. Jeremiah Crawford, 6-foot-5, 315 pounds, thirteen starts at tackle at Tennessee, a Panthers camp in 2024, and a practice-roster stint in Winnipeg this fall, is a length-and-mass bet. Temi Ajirotutu is 6-foot-5, 310, a 45-start guard/tackle at Villanova, and according to Pro Football Focus he surrendered 52 pressures and six sacks in his college career. Against FCS competition, that pressure number is a red flag, not a résumé line. Neither player is a Week 1 answer. Both are the right body type for a team that just got mauled inside.

What the Rest of the Class Says About the Offence

The most interesting name in the batch is La'Vontae Shenault, and not for the reason the highlight-reel crowd thinks. The 6-foot-2, 195-pound receiver has a strange arc: eleven games at Colorado across multiple suspensions, fourteen catches at Alabama State in 2022, no college football at all in 2023 or 2024, then a 75-catch, 1,044-yard, five-touchdown season at Northeastern State, an NCAA Division II program in Oklahoma, in 2025. That's a 25-year-old with two lost seasons and one year of production against small-school corners.

He is also, functionally, a traits lottery ticket at the exact position Winnipeg has lacked: a boundary receiver who can win contested and push vertical. The Bombers' passing game has been built on underneath efficiency and run-after-catch for three years, and seventh in yards per play is the receipt. Shenault's return background, thirteen punt returns for 74 yards at Northeastern State, four for 38 at Colorado, is modest, but it signals the special-teams value that gets a developmental receiver onto the roster. He is a camp body with a real shot, not a solution.

Defensively, the additions of Norell Pollard, Nicholas Lenon and Kris Trinidad up front plus Virdel Edwards II and Clay Fields III in the secondary follow the same template: churn the American pipeline, backfill the ratio, keep the defence cheap and deep. That defence was tied for fifth and the special teams ranked first, which is precisely the problem. Winnipeg won games on hidden yards and field position while the offence ranked seventh in the categories that decide playoff football.

The 2027 Mandate

Condell is under contract through 2027, and O'Shea expects him back. That's the first coordinator continuity this team has had since 2024, and it will be worth exactly nothing unless the protection gets fixed and the receiver room gets a vertical element. Continuity of voice on a roster that can't hold a pocket produces the same seventh-place numbers with a nicer explanation attached.

O'Shea said it plainly: "You need to score points. Absolutely, [the lack of offensive scoring is] concerning." The concern is warranted, but the diagnosis has to be right. This isn't a play-calling crisis. It's an interior-offensive-line and boundary-receiver crisis that has now outlasted three play callers. Until that changes, the Bombers will keep finishing seventh in everything that matters, and the defence and special teams will keep covering for it.
