Bombers' Defense Resurrects Identity, But Collaros Injury Casts Shadow

September 13, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Defensive Recalibration

The Winnipeg Blue Bombers' 19-6 victory over the Saskatchewan Roughriders on Saturday was not just a win—it was a statement. The defense that had been gashed for 30-plus points in three of their previous five games suddenly looked like the unit that anchored three straight Grey Cup appearances. The numbers tell the story: 278 yards of net offense allowed, five turnovers forced, and a season-best three pass knockdowns from cornerback Evan Holm. But the deeper film reveals a schematic shift that went beyond mere execution.

Defensive coordinator Jordan Younger dialed up more press-man coverage on early downs than we've seen all season. According to CFL statistics, Winnipeg blitzed on only 11 of Saskatchewan's 62 offensive snaps, yet generated pressure on 14 of Trevor Harris's 38 dropbacks. The key was the return of veteran defensive back Redha Kramdi, who played his first game since suffering a knee injury in Week 4. Kramdi's presence allowed the Bombers to run their "Cover 1 Robber" scheme—a single-high safety with Kramdi as the robber underneath—which took away Harris's favorite crossing routes. The result: Harris completed just 19-of-36 passes for 178 yards, with a 4.9 yards-per-attempt average, his lowest of the season.

The film shows Kramdi's impact on the game's first turnover. On Saskatchewan's second possession, Kramdi read the run-pass option from Harris, attacked the mesh point, and punched the ball out of running back D'Sean Mimbs's grasp. It wasn't a blind gamble—Kramdi had diagnosed the tendency from film study, noting that Mimbs carried the ball loosely on outside zone runs. That forced fumble set the tone for a defense that finished with a 28.6 percent pressure rate, according to Pro Football Focus data. Defensive tackle Jake Ceresna's sack in the second quarter was a direct result of the stunts the Bombers ran on 3rd-and-5, with Ceresna looping inside while end Willie Jefferson crashed outside. It was a designed pressure that exploited Saskatchewan's rookie right guard.

Offensive Line Fractures and Play-Calling Conservatism

While the defense soared, the offense sputtered—and not just because of the quarterback injury. The Bombers allowed five sacks on the day, with four coming on Zach Collaros before his exit. The first sack was a whiff by running back Brady Oliveira, who missed a chip block on defensive end Seyi Oladipo Jr. But the other three were more concerning. Left tackle Stanley Bryant, a future Hall of Famer, was beaten twice by Oladipo on inside counter moves—a rarity for a player who has allowed just two sacks all season entering the game. The film shows Bryant's set depth was too shallow on those plays, allowing the defender to get into his chest and shed.

Offensive coordinator Buck Pierce's play-calling didn't help. On 2nd-and-long situations (7+ yards), the Bombers ran the ball on 11 of 19 such downs, a 58 percent rate that is the highest in the CFL this season. This conservative approach put Collaros in predictable passing situations on 3rd-and-medium, where Saskatchewan's defense could tee off. The Bombers' only touchdown came on a 22-yard screen pass to Oliveira in the second quarter, a play that was beautifully set up by a play-action fake off the same formation they had used on three consecutive running plays. But that creativity was rare. Pierce called just four play-action passes all game, despite the fact that Collaros has a 118.2 passer rating on play-action this season—the best in the league.

The offensive line's struggles also exposed a deeper issue: the loss of center Chris Kolankowski to a season-ending injury in Week 8 has forced a reshuffle that has destabilized the interior. Backup center Liam Dobson was beaten on the sack that forced Collaros's interception in the third quarter. On that play, Dobson failed to pick up a twist between defensive tackles, leaving Oladipo free to hit Collaros as he threw. The interception was a direct result of the pressure, not a poor decision. This is a trend: over the last four games, the Bombers have allowed 14 sacks, the second-most in the league. The offensive line is no longer a strength, and Pierce's play-calling has not adjusted to compensate.

The Collaros Conundrum and the Backup Question

Zach Collaros's injury—a suspected upper-body issue after landing on his head and neck while attempting a tackle on his interception return—is the most critical storyline. The 38-year-old has now missed games in three consecutive seasons due to neck injuries, and this latest scare comes just weeks after he returned from a four-game absence due to whiplash. The film of the play shows Collaros making a poor decision to chase the interception, but his competitive nature is also what makes him effective. The Bombers' offense without him is a different animal.

Backup Dru Brown, who entered in relief and went 4-of-5 for 46 yards, is a capable game manager but not a downfield threat. Brown's average depth of target on his five attempts was just 6.2 yards, compared to Collaros's 9.1 yards per attempt on the day. If Collaros misses extended time, the Bombers will likely lean even more heavily on the running game and short passing—a formula that worked in the fourth quarter against Saskatchewan, but that will not suffice against elite defenses like Toronto or Montreal. The Bombers' schedule over the next three weeks includes games against the Alouettes and Argonauts, both of whom rank in the top three in defensive efficiency. Without Collaros, the offense could regress to the 2023 version that averaged just 21.3 points per game in his absence.

But the bigger question is whether the Bombers can sustain this defensive performance. The unit's success on Saturday was built on a specific game plan that exploited Saskatchewan's predictable tendencies.
