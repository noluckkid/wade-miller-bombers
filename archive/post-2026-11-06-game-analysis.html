Philpot Is the Whole Chessboard: How Winnipeg Must Solve Montreal's Slot Machine

November 06, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The 187-Yard Problem

Start with the number that should keep Winnipeg's defensive staff up all week: 187. That's Tyson Philpot's per-game average across two meetings with the Bombers, capped by a 207-yard, 11-catch demolition in Week 18. Winnipeg finished the regular season allowing the fewest passing yards in the league, and both things are true — which tells you the Bombers' coverage shell is structurally vulnerable to one specific player, not to passing offenses in general.

The film explains why. Jordan Younger's defence lives in pattern-match quarters and Cover 3 with a rotating safety, and it's built to cap explosive plays outside the numbers. Against Montreal, that means Philpot gets a steady diet of soft inside leverage in the slot — the exact leverage he eats. Noel Thorpe's offence moves him constantly: reduced splits to shrink the throwing window, bunch and stack releases to muddy the match rules, and a heavy dose of option routes on third-and-medium where Philpot reads the leverage defender and breaks the opposite way. When Winnipeg has tried to pattern-match him with a linebacker or a safety rotating late, Thorpe has countered with rail routes and deep crossers behind the rotation.

The fix is uncomfortable. Bracket him — a cloud corner with a safety over the top — and Montreal will run Austin Mack and the tight-end package into the vacated middle. Play him man with a jam and no help, and you're asking a corner to survive a 207-yard receiver in space. The Bombers' best realistic answer is selective: press him at the line to disrupt timing on early downs, keep the rotation honest, and make someone else beat them on second-and-long. They haven't done it yet.

The Quarterback Fork Changes the Call Sheet

Zach Collaros was a non-participant at Thursday's practice and Dru Brown took first-team reps, which all but confirms Brown starts the East Final. O'Shea won't say it — "I don't see any point in talking about the roster before we have to" — but the depth chart Friday morning will. That matters schematically, because the Bombers call two different games for two different quarterbacks.

With Collaros, Winnipeg is an anticipation offence: deep play-action off split-zone, aggressive boundary shots, and a willingness to throw into tight windows on second-and-8. That 44-28 August win was the Collaros blueprint — Montreal's pressure got punished by quick answers over the top. Brown is a movement-and-checkdown quarterback. In the East Semi-Final he went 18-of-30 for 206 yards with two touchdowns and two interceptions, and the game-winner to Nic Demski inside the final minute came on a designed progression, not improvisation. Brown's legs extend drives; his eyes don't threaten the whole field the way Collaros' do.

Expect Winnipeg to lean harder on 12-personnel, condensed formations, and run-pass options that keep Brown's reads on one side of the field — plus more bootleg and naked keepers to move the pocket away from Montreal's interior pressure. The risk: Montreal's defence is built to bait exactly those half-field throws. Brown's two picks last week came on throws where the second-level defender read his eyes.

Oliveira and the Math of Possession

If Philpot is the problem, Brady Oliveira is the solution — not because he out-scores him, but because he keeps him on the bench. The two-time Most Outstanding Canadian ran for 106 yards on 16 carries in Week 13 and got stuffed in the October meeting, a 37-10 Montreal win in which the Bombers abandoned the run early. That abandonment is the losing formula. Winnipeg's run game is inside zone and duo with a pin-and-pull wrinkle off the backside, and it works best when Oliveira gets 15-plus carries and the offence stays on schedule.

Montreal's front is aggressive and twist-heavy, which means the Bombers' interior trio has to pass off stunts cleanly to reach the second level. When Winnipeg has stayed patient, Oliveira's yards after contact have punished Montreal's linebackers. When they've chased points, Montreal's edge rushers have teed off on a one-dimensional offence. A 30-minute time-of-possession game is a defensive strategy in this matchup.

The Matchup That Decides It

Third-and-medium in the red zone. Philpot lives there, and Winnipeg's red-zone coverage has been its most conservative all year — off-coverage, four-down rush, tackle-the-catch. That's a recipe for a 12-yard spot route on repeat. If Younger commits to pressing Philpot inside the 20 and trusts his corners, the Bombers have a path. If he doesn't, Montreal moves the chains and Collaros' ribs become a moot point.

The other swing factor is field position. Winnipeg's special teams have quietly been the difference in tight games, and in a November game at Molson Stadium — cold, wind, a natural-turf surface that makes cutting harder — hidden yards matter double. The Bombers won the August meeting by 16 with Collaros throwing over the top. They lost the October meeting by 27 with Brown checking down. The tape says the margin between those outcomes is a coverage leverage decision and 12 carries. O'Shea has three days to pick the right ones.
