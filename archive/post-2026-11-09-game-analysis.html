Winnipeg's East Final Autopsy: A Protection Bust, a Stale Scheme, QB Limbo

November 09, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Unblocked Man: How One Protection Rule Cost Four Games

Go back to the first week of July in Hamilton. Reggie Stubblefield, a strong-side linebacker, walked down late and came clean off the edge for a seven-yard sack of Zach Collaros. No chip, no check-release, no sliding lineman. According to Collaros's own year-end press conference, that hit — a neck injury — cost him the next four games and effectively killed the two-year extension the two sides had been negotiating in the first month of the season.

Watch the protection rules on that rep and you see the flaw that defined Winnipeg's year. The Bombers run a half-slide front, with the running back scanning inside-out and the quarterback responsible for the most dangerous free rusher. When a CFL defense walks an S-side linebacker down late — after the protection call is locked — the answer has to be a hot adjustment: a sight-adjust on the slot, a quick throw to the vacated zone, or a back who redirects. Winnipeg had none of the three. Stubblefield was unaccounted for because the rules, not the athletes, failed. For a 38-year-old passer who has taken more hits than any quarterback in the league over the last six seasons, a protection system that leaves a late-walking linebacker free is not bad luck. It is a design liability.

21-of-27, 228 Yards, and the Missing Explosive

Now the East Final in Montreal, a 33-23 loss to the Alouettes. Collaros finished 21-of-27 for 228 yards — an 8.4 yards-per-attempt number that looks efficient until you separate it from the game script. Twenty-seven attempts and 228 yards means the Bombers never stressed the field vertically. The completion rate is a symptom, not a strength: the offense lived on quick game, perimeter screens and five-to-seven-yard option routes, with Montreal's second level sitting on everything underneath.

This is a sea change from the Mike O'Shea offenses that won two Grey Cups. Those teams married wide-zone runs with play-action and deep crossers off boot, and Collaros's off-schedule creativity was the multiplier. The 2025 version leaned horizontal because the protection couldn't hold long enough to let deep concepts develop — and because Collaros's three separate injuries this season (neck, head, ribs) robbed the offense of the second-reaction throws that used to make this scheme go. When your quarterback can't extend, your play-action has to win on timing alone, and Winnipeg's timing concepts were the most static in the division.

Thorpe's Pressure Menu vs. Static Reads

The matchup that decided the East Final was Noel Thorpe's pressure menu against Winnipeg's pre-snap picture. Thorpe is the CFL's most aggressive pressure designer: simulated pressures with dropping defensive ends, late-walking linebackers, and second-level blitzes that show A-gap and rush off the tackle's outside shoulder. Against a quarterback who can't escape and an offense that doesn't sight-adjust, that menu compounds.

Count the reps where Montreal rushed four but showed six, and you'll find the Bombers sliding protection to the wrong side, then throwing hot to a receiver running a route designed for man coverage against a defense sitting in a three-deep shell. Davis Alexander and the Als' offense, meanwhile, exploited Winnipeg's own static tendencies. Richie Hall's defense is built on disguise and front-four pressure, but it's a bend-don't-break system that concedes the underneath throws, and Alexander took them all day while the Bombers' edge rushers got home too slowly to punish him. When the front four doesn't win in under three seconds, the zone behind it has to tackle in space — and Winnipeg's second level got picked apart on the perimeter.

Reese, Discipline, and the Cost of Emotional Leakage

There's a scheme story buried in the David Reese accusation, too. Alexander alleged — multiple times, per his postgame comments — that the defensive lineman taunted him about his father, who died of lung cancer in 2024. Tyson Philpot called it "super disrespectful," and Jason Maas acknowledged his quarterback was "very fired up." Whatever the league does with it, the football takeaway is real: a rotational defensive lineman who loses his emotional discipline on the field is a gap-integrity and penalty risk on every snap. Winnipeg's defense won the discipline battle statistically all year, but a player who can be baited into running his mouth instead of his rush is a liability in a Thorpe-style pressure environment where a single missed fit turns a three-yard checkdown into a twelve-yard chain-mover.

The Contract That Wasn't, and What Comes Next

Collaros said the two sides were talking about a deal — possibly two years, which would take him through age 40 — through the first four weeks, and haven't touched base since. He's a pending free agent, undecided, in no hurry. That's not just a roster question; it's the central schematic fork for this franchise.

If Collaros returns, the Bombers owe him a rebuilt protection system: hot adjustments installed as core rules, a back who can redirect on late-walking linebackers, and more vertical concepts off play-action so the offense isn't capped at eight yards a throw. If he doesn't, they need a transition plan that changes the scheme's spine rather than plugging a young arm into a 2019 playbook. Either way, the tape says the same thing. Winnipeg didn't lose the East Final because Montreal is more talented. They lost it because their protection broke, their offense compressed, and nobody on the sideline had a counter. The contract is the headline. The X's and O's are the real story.
