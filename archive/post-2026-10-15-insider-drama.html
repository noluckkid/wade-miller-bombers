The SAM Carousel: Inside the Bombers' Quiet Rot at 8-8

October 15, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The SAM Carousel Is a Confession, Not a Rotation

When the Winnipeg Blue Bombers released their depth chart on Thursday morning ahead of Friday's 9:30 p.m. ET kickoff in Edmonton, the headline was transactional: Canadian defensive back Jaylen Smith to the one-game injured list with a hip injury, Michael Griffin II back into the starting lineup at strong-side linebacker, Canadian linebacker Lane Novak promoted off the practice roster to fill the vacated roster spot.

Read it again. That is not a depth chart. That is an admission.

Smith, 24, was a second-round pick in the 2025 CFL Draft out of North Texas. He has made seven starts at SAM this season and recorded 46 total tackles. Griffin, in his third year in Winnipeg, has made nine starts at the same position, with 39 defensive tackles, one sack and one forced fumble. Sixteen starts, two players, one job. In Week 20. With a playoff berth on the line.

In Mike O'Shea's defense, SAM is not a plug-and-play spot. It is the position that carries the most pre-snap communication burden — setting the edge against the run, matching the slot receiver, timing the blitz. Rotating it is survivable. Splitting it 9-7 in a season where you're 8-8 is a verdict. The Bombers spent a second-round pick on Smith specifically to take this job, and in October he still hasn't taken it. Griffin, meanwhile, is a known quantity: 51 career regular-season games, 139 defensive tackles, four sacks, four pass knockdowns. He is a floor. He has always been a floor. When your floor and your ceiling are dead even in Week 20, the evaluation failed somewhere back in July.

And then there's Novak. Balgonie, Sask. Fifth-round pick in 2025. Ten regular-season games, four special teams tackles. Promoting him is not a football decision; it is ratio bookkeeping — a Canadian linebacker backfilling a Canadian defensive back's roster spot so the national ratio doesn't break. Teams manage the ratio in Week 20 when they are 8-8. Teams that are 12-4 manage it in August.

Four Fumbles and a Pattern O'Shea Can't Explain Away

The football case against these Bombers is not complicated. It is two games against Edmonton, decided by eight total points.

Week 4, Princess Auto Stadium: Winnipeg fumbles four times in the first half and loses 23-18. Week 12, Commonwealth Stadium: Brady Oliveira rushes for 118 yards and Winnipeg loses 22-19 anyway. A combined margin of eight points across two losses to the same opponent. Flip either one and the Bombers are 10-6, hosting a playoff game, and nobody is talking about Calgary's result on Saturday night.

Four fumbles in a half is not variance. Variance is one. Four is a systemic ball-security failure, and it happened in a game Winnipeg lost by five points — meaning the turnovers were not absorbed, they were the margin. O'Shea is the longest-tenured head coach in the CFL and has banked a decade of credibility with nine consecutive playoff appearances and five Grey Cup trips. That credibility is real and it is earned. It is also the reason nobody inside the building has been forced to answer for a turnover cluster that has now defined a season.

Watch the fourth-quarter decision-making on Friday. If the Bombers get to the fourth quarter within one score in Edmonton, the coaching staff's clock management and situational aggression will be the single most scrutinized thing in the building — because they have already lost two games to this opponent by a field goal, and the difference between 8-8 and 10-6 lives entirely in those margins.

Oliveira Is Carrying an Offense That Can't Separate

Look at the scorelines. 23-18. 22-19. This is an offense that scores 18 or 19 points in its most important games and asks its defense to win by a field goal. That is not a balanced team. That is a team with one functioning phase and a structural problem everywhere else.

Oliveira's 118-yard night in Week 12 is the tell. When your feature back rips off 118 yards on the road and you still lose, the run game is not the issue — the run game is the alibi. Winnipeg has built a roster that requires the ground attack to be perfect in order to compensate for a passing game that cannot generate separation in tight games. That is a construction problem, and construction problems belong to the general manager, not the coordinator.

There is a human cost to this too. A running back who puts 118 on the board in a loss absorbs the physical punishment of a 20-carry night and gets nothing for it. Do that four or five times in a season and the locker room starts doing math — about who is being asked to carry the load and who is being protected by the scheme. Those conversations don't happen in press conferences. They happen in the training room in November.

The Window Is Closing and the Building Knows It

Context matters here, and the context is brutal. Nine straight playoff appearances. Five Grey Cup appearances. Two championships. And then 2025: the first time in six years Winnipeg missed the CFL championship game, eliminated by Montreal in the East Semi-Final as a crossover team. Their last playoff win was 2024 against Saskatchewan.

That is a decade-long contention window that is now visibly narrowing, and the roster reflects it. When a franchise spends ten years paying for the top of the market, the bill arrives in the form of restructured veteran deals, deferred money, and a depth chart that gets thin exactly where it got expensive. The Bombers have been managing that bill for three seasons. The SAM carousel, the ratio gymnastics, the practice-roster promotions in Week 20 — none of that is bad luck. It is the arithmetic of sustained success in a hard cap league.

Which brings us to Friday. Winnipeg controls its own destiny: beat Edmonton and they're in. Lose, and they need Calgary to fall to B.C. on Saturday night.
