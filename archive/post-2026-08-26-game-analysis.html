The Bombers' Red Zone Rot: Winnipeg's Inability to Finish is a Scheme Problem

August 26, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Numbers Don't Lie: A Season of Squandered Opportunities

Through 10 games, the Winnipeg Blue Bombers sit at 5-5, a record that flatters an offence ranked seventh in the CFL at 24.7 points per game. But the most damning statistic isn't the points per game — it's the red zone conversion rate. Winnipeg has generated just 29 red zone opportunities, ranked eighth in the league, and their touchdown rate inside the 20 has been abysmal. The 22-19 loss to the Edmonton Elks on October 4th was the nadir: three trips inside the 20, zero touchdowns, and a season-first bagel from the opponent's 20-yard line and in.

Compare that to Edmonton, who lead the league with 42 red zone trips. The Elks aren't just getting more opportunities — they're manufacturing them through a more aggressive downfield passing attack and a commitment to stretching the field horizontally. Winnipeg's offence, by contrast, has become predictable in the red zone, relying on condensed formations that allow defences to play man coverage with safety help over the top. The result is a unit that settles for Sergio Castillo field goals — including a 15-yard chip shot after two failed passes from the seven-yard line — and a third-and-three gamble from the five that went nowhere.

Film Breakdown: The Edmonton Game — A Masterclass in Misfires

Let's go to the tape from the October 4th loss. On the first red zone trip, Winnipeg faced a second-and-goal from the seven. The Bombers came out in 12 personnel (one back, two tight ends) with trips to the right. The Elks countered with press-man coverage and a single-high safety. The play call was a shallow cross to Tim White, who was running from the slot. The issue? The route spacing was compressed — White's break point was within three yards of the boundary receiver's stem, allowing the cornerback to pass off the route to the safety while the linebacker underneath squeezed the throwing lane. Quarterback Zach Collaros had nowhere to go with the football and was forced to throw it away.

The third-down gamble from the five was even more telling. Winnipeg went with an empty backfield, four-wide set, and called a fade-stop to the boundary. The Elks showed blitz pre-snap, then dropped into Cover-2. The boundary cornerback jammed the receiver at the line, disrupting the timing, and the safety drove on the underneath route. Collaros had to hold the ball an extra beat, and the pressure got home. White's post-game quote — "We had man coverage on the third-down gamble, and we've just got to execute better, set guys up in positions" — is a polite way of saying the play design didn't create any natural separation against man coverage.

The second red zone trip was worse. Winnipeg drove to the Elks' 12-yard line, faced a second-and-five, and called a run-pass option. The offensive line zone-blocked to the left, but the Elks' defensive tackle crashed down hard, forcing running back Brady Oliveira to bounce outside. The pulling guard couldn't get to the second level, and the linebacker scraped over the top to make the tackle for a two-yard gain. On third down, instead of going for it, the Bombers settled for a 20-yard Castillo field goal. That's a four-point swing in a game they lost by three.

Scheme and Play-Calling: The Predictability Problem

The deeper issue is schematic. Offensive coordinator Buck Pierce has built an offence that thrives between the 30s — Winnipeg ranks in the top half of the league in net offence and first downs — but the play-calling tightens up dramatically inside the 20. The Bombers are running the ball on 52% of their red zone plays, but their success rate on those runs is just 38%, according to CFL Stats Hub. The problem is that Pierce is calling inside zone runs into the teeth of stacked boxes, rather than using jet sweeps, screens, or play-action to create conflict for the defence.

There's also a personnel issue. The Bombers are missing a true red zone threat — a big-bodied receiver who can win 50-50 balls in the end zone. Kenny Lawler is a possession receiver, not a jump-ball specialist. Tim White is a yards-after-catch guy, not a fade-route target. The tight end position has been a non-factor in the red zone, with Winnipeg's tight ends combining for just two touchdowns all season. When you don't have a mismatch creator, you have to scheme your way open — and the current route combinations aren't doing that.

Head coach Mike O'Shea's response to the red zone struggles has been characteristically stoic: "We're down to some finite, fine fixtures, little details, honing the craft." But the film doesn't lie. The Bombers are running the same red zone concepts they ran in 2021 and 2022, when they had Andrew Harris in the backfield and a healthier offensive line. The league has caught up. Defensive coordinators are now playing Cover-2 and quarters in the red zone, taking away the shallow cross and the back-shoulder fade that used to be Winnipeg's bread and butter.

The Path Forward: What Needs to Change

The Bombers have 10 games left in the regular season, and the schedule doesn't get easier. They face the B.C. Lions twice, the Toronto Argonauts, and a rematch with Edmonton. If they're going to make a playoff push, the red zone offence has to improve — and that starts with scheme. Pierce needs to incorporate more play-action bootlegs in the red zone, which would force defences to respect the run and open up the flat and the corner of the end zone. He also needs to use more bunch formations to create natural picks and rubs against man coverage.

There's also a case for more aggressive fourth-down decision-making. The Bombers are 5-5, and they're not good enough to settle for field goals against elite offences. O'Shea's conservative nature has cost them points in close games — the decision to kick a 20-yard field goal on second-and-five from the 12 was a white-flag moment.
