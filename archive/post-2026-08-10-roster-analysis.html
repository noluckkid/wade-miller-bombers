Bombers' Roster Decisions: Collaros Return Masks a Fragile Core

August 10, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Quarterback Hierarchy Resets

The most consequential transaction of the Winnipeg Blue Bombers' bye week didn't involve the CFL transactions wire. It happened on the practice field Sunday, when Zach Collaros took first-team reps for the first time since July 5th. That's when Hamilton Tiger-Cats defender Reggie Stubblefield drilled Collaros in the neck, forcing him from the game and sending Taylor Elgersma into a relief appearance that eventually secured a win. Collaros missed the next four games, landing on the injury list and leaving the offense to Dru Brown. Brown delivered two wins—over Toronto and Ottawa—then lost back-to-back contests to Calgary and B.C., the latter a 35-19 disaster in which the defense allowed 377 rushing yards.

Collaros's return is a classic roster-move lateral: the club activated its starter from the injury list, demoting a backup who had a 2-2 record. But Brown's performance should worry Winnipeg more than it reassures. The Bombers didn't lose those two games because Brown played badly; they lost because the defense could not stop anyone. Collaros himself acknowledged the decision was driven by medical staff—"solely based on Al and Dr. Craton and me"—which says more about the organization's risk tolerance than its ambition. The Bombers did not make a single quarterback acquisition during Collaros's absence. That's a tell: the front office was comfortable with Brown, and now it's comfortable with an unproven 2026 rotation that still has Elgersma as the emergency third-stringer.

The Line of Scrimmage Shuffle

Two more names reappeared at practice Sunday: right tackle Jarell Broxton and defensive tackle Cam Lawson. Broxton's return is significant for protection—the Bombers have shuffled their offensive line regularly this year, and Brown took hits in losses to Calgary and B.C. Lawson's return is arguably more critical. The defensive tackle rotation has been gashed without him, and the 377-yard rushing total against B.C. represented the positional failure of a unit that was supposed to be the team's anchor. The bye week functioned as a de facto transaction window: Williams activated, Broxton activated, Lawson activated. But Winnipeg used the actual transaction wire for nothing of note. No veteran defensive back was added. No interior offensive lineman was brought in. The club's front office apparently trusts its depth charts over the available market.

That trust is statistically risky. Winnipeg has 17 sacks through eight games, tied for second in the CFL and a sudden reversal from last year's league-worst 23 total. Willie Jefferson and Jake Ceresna each have six sacks, tied for the league lead. Those numbers mask a run-defense unit that is bleeding yardage at catastrophic rates. The return of Lawson helps, but no single defensive tackle fixes a scheme that allowed a rival to rush for nearly 400 yards. The Bombers essentially bet that Broxton and Lawson, plus Collaros, will cover the cracks. The next four weeks will determine whether that's a functional roster strategy or an act of nostalgia.

The Workload Counterargument

On the offensive side, the Bombers are leaning harder than ever on Brady Oliveira. He leads the CFL in carries and rushing yards, and his workload has spiked: 33 carries for 235 yards in the last two games, versus 24 for 176 in the two prior contests. That increase is a roster decision, not a coincidence—with Brown at quarterback, the coaching staff pressed the run game to protect him. But the data suggests Oliveira's production isn't translating to wins the way it used to. From 2021 through 2024, Winnipeg went 16-1 in his 100-yard rushing games. Over the last two seasons, they are 3-4. That is a damning indication that the surrounding roster has declined, even as the star running back maintains his performance.

The Bombers' cap situation is the underlying reason they didn't go shopping when the bye week presented a opportunity. Winnipeg has committed significant dollars to Collaros, Oliveira, and the defensive line, which limits flexibility for mid-season additions. The front office instead chose the injured-list path: let the rested veterans return, hope the medical staff's clearance translates to on-field stability, and trust that a 4-4 record will improve without a single external reinforcement. The 2026 CFL trade market was quiet, but the Bombers' internal quiet was still a choice. They are betting recovery, not acquisition.

The Unforgiving Math Ahead

Collaros's neck checks out. Broxton and Lawson are back. The bye week cleaned the roster of the most glaring red crosses. But the Bombers still have the same defensive backfield that gave up 377 rushing yards, the same offensive line that needed Broxton to return, and the same front office that elected not to seek help. The next eight games will test whether the activated veterans are enough to convert those 23 sacks into wins. The transaction wire might as well have been empty for Winnipeg—and that is the most telling roster move of all.
