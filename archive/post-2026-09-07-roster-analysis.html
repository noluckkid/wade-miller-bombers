The Bombers' Roster Roulette: Aging Stars, Thin Depth, and a Fading Window

September 07, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Collaros Conundrum

Zach Collaros is 38 years old, has missed four games with a neck injury, and is completing passes at a career-best rate when healthy. The Bombers' offensive coordinator Tommy Condell says the veteran has "the keys to the Maserati" — but the question is whether the Bombers can afford the insurance payments. Collaros's numbers when healthy are solid: 140-of-196 (71.4 percent) for 1,750 yards, eight touchdowns, and four interceptions. His 26-of-32, 324-yard, two-touchdown performance against Montreal was vintage. But the neck injury that cost him four games is a recurring concern for a quarterback who turns 39 next season.

The Bombers have structured Collaros's deal to push money down the road, but that cap hit is coming due. With the CFL salary cap hovering around $5.5 million, committing roughly 10 percent of the cap to a 38-year-old quarterback with a neck injury is a gamble. The question isn't whether Collaros can still play — it's whether the Bombers can afford the roster depth they sacrifice to keep him. The team's decision to bring back Condell, who coached Collaros in Hamilton, was a bet on continuity over innovation. So far, the offence ranks seventh in the CFL in net yardage, yards per play, and passing — a damning indictment of that bet.

The Defensive Backfield Carousel

Ridge Texada was dressed for only his second career CFL game in the Labour Day Classic. He got fooled on a double-move, gave up a bust in coverage, and was part of a secondary that surrendered 32 points. This is what happens when a team's defensive backfield depth chart looks like a MASH unit. The Bombers have cycled through defensive backs all season. Jaiden Woodbey missed a tackle on a 72-yard catch-and-run from Daniel Wiebe. Evan Holm took a costly face-masking penalty that extended a Saskatchewan touchdown drive. The unit held the Roughriders to 13 points through three quarters, but fatigue and mental errors in the fourth quarter and overtime undid the effort.

This is a roster construction problem. The Bombers have invested heavily in their front seven — Willie Jefferson, Adam Bighill, and the defensive line eat up significant cap space — leaving the secondary to be filled with bargain-bin signings and practice squad promotions. When injuries hit, the drop-off is steep. Texada's promotion from the practice squad was a desperation move, not a strategic one. The team's failure to address defensive back depth in free agency and the draft has left them exposed. Saskatchewan's Trevor Harris exploited that exposure repeatedly, and the Bombers' inability to get stops in overtime sealed their fate.

Cap Management and the Aging Core

The Bombers' core has been together for five Grey Cup appearances. That continuity is valuable, but it comes at a cost. Veterans like Jefferson, Bighill, Collaros, and offensive linemen Stanley Bryant and Pat Neufeld are all on the wrong side of 30, and their contracts reflect past production rather than future value. The offence currently sits seventh in the CFL in net yardage, yards per play, and passing. That's not a blip — that's a trend. The Bombers have struggled to replace production from departed receivers and have leaned on Collaros to carry an offence that lacks explosive playmakers.

Brady Oliveira remains a workhorse, but the Bombers' receiving corps has been inconsistent. The team's decision to let key receivers walk in free agency and rely on unproven depth has backfired. The result is an offence that ranks in the bottom half of the league in nearly every meaningful category. The cap math is brutal: Jefferson's deal, Bighill's deal, Collaros's deal, and the offensive line contracts leave little room for mid-season acquisitions. The Bombers have been forced to scrape the bottom of the transaction wire, signing castoffs and practice squad players to fill holes. That's not a sustainable model for a team with Grey Cup aspirations.

What's Next: Trade Deadline and Practice Squad Moves

The CFL trade deadline is approaching, and the Bombers need to decide whether they're buyers or sellers. At 4-6, they're on the playoff bubble. If they believe Collaros can stay healthy and the defence can hold up, they might add a receiver or defensive back. If not, they could be sellers, moving veterans for draft picks and younger assets. The practice squad has been a revolving door. The Bombers have promoted and demoted players all season, trying to find answers at defensive back and receiver. This is a sign of poor roster planning — the team didn't adequately address depth in the offseason and is now paying the price.

The Bombers' window isn't closed, but it's closing. The roster decisions made in the next few weeks will determine whether this team makes one more run or begins a rebuild. The cap is tight, the core is aging, and the depth is thin. That's a dangerous combination in a league where parity is the norm. The Labour Day Classic loss was a microcosm of the season: flashes of brilliance undermined by structural weaknesses. The Bombers need to make bold moves — whether that means trading a veteran for picks, signing a free agent, or promoting from within — before the window slams shut.
