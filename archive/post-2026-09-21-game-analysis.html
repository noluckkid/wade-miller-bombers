The Bombers' Protection Problem: Winnipeg's Offence Is Out of Answers

September 21, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Protection Problem: A Five-Man Slide That Isn't Holding

Start with the obvious: Winnipeg's offensive line is not the 2021 unit, and Jason Hogan's play-calling has been shaped around that reality rather than in spite of it. Stanley Bryant is gone. Jermarcus Hardrick is in Regina. What's left is a group that has rotated tackles — Eric Lofton, Kendall Randolph, and at times a sixth lineman — and an interior anchored by Chris Kolankowski, Liam Dobson and Pat Neufeld that wins on double-teams but struggles when asked to pass-set in isolation against a wide-nine edge.

Watch the film and the tell is structural. On early downs, Winnipeg has been keeping a running back or tight end in to chip on roughly a third of dropbacks — call it 30 to 35 percent by my charting — which functionally reduces Zach Collaros to two-man route combinations on the back end. When you only release three, a defence can bracket Nic Demski in the slot and roll a safety over the top of the boundary receiver without any real cost. Collaros is a rhythm and anticipation thrower, not a creator; his completion rate and yards per attempt fall off sharply once his first read is taken away and the pocket narrows. That's not a quarterback decline story. It's a protection math story.

The counter Hogan has used is 12-personnel — two tight ends, one back — which buys an extra blocker but costs a receiver. Defences have answered by walking a safety down into the box and playing single-high behind it, daring Winnipeg to win outside the numbers. The Bombers' answer has been play-action off the run game, and it remains their most efficient concept: Collaros throwing off a hard sell of the inside zone to a crossing route behind the linebackers. But play-action only works if the run game is respected, and that's the second problem.

Brady Oliveira and the Vanishing Under-Centre Run Game

Brady Oliveira is a one-cut, downhill back. He's at his best on duo and inside zone with a lead blocker, pressing the A-gap, forcing the linebacker to commit, and cutting off his blocks. Winnipeg's most efficient run concepts in 2026 have been exactly that — and the staff has been getting away from them.

Why? Because defences are loading the box. With a safety at eight or nine yards and a nickel walked up over the slot, the Bombers see light boxes only when they spread the field, and heavy boxes when they bunch. The response has been more outside zone and pin-and-pull, which asks Oliveira to reach the edge and read a moving front. That is not his game. His yards before contact have dropped noticeably on those concepts, and the offence has been left in second-and-eight instead of second-and-five — which is precisely the down-and-distance that makes max protection necessary on the next snap. It's a loop, and it's self-reinforcing.

The fix isn't exotic. It's more under-centre, more gap-scheme runs with a pulling guard, and more use of a true fullback or H-back as a lead blocker rather than as a pass protector. Winnipeg has the personnel to do it. What it hasn't had is the willingness to stay with it when the first two carries go for two yards.

Jordan Younger's Simulated Pressure and the Third-Down Math

Defensively, Jordan Younger's unit is still built on disguise, and the disguise is still good. Winnipeg has leaned heavily on simulated pressure — rushing four while dropping a defensive end into a hook or curl-flat zone, or rushing three and walking a linebacker up late. Willie Jefferson remains the engine, but his role has shifted: he's less a pure speed-to-power edge winner on true dropbacks and more a looper and game-stunter, which is why you see so many tackle-end twists and long sticks on obvious passing downs.

The coverage behind it is pattern-match. Deatrick Nichols in the slot is the club's best man defender and gets isolated depending on formation. Evan Holm and Jamal Parker play outside with safety help from Brandon Alexander. The scheme's soft spot is the same one it's always been: intermediate crossers and seam routes against single-high, especially when the rush doesn't get home. On third-and-six through third-and-nine, Winnipeg has been content to rush four and play coverage, and patient quarterbacks have been willing to take the checkdown and convert with their legs.

The Matchup That Decides the West

Here's the thing about the West race: Edmonton sits first despite a loss in Toronto in which the Elks held the ball for nearly 39 minutes and still lost by five. That's a team that wins the possession battle and loses the explosive-play battle — and that is a matchup Winnipeg can exploit. Cody Fajardo threw two interceptions into the end zone in that game. Against a Younger defence that thrives on late rotation and baiting throws, Fajardo's willingness to force the ball into tight windows is the single biggest edge on the Bombers' remaining schedule.

The problem is the other side of the ball. If Winnipeg can't run the ball on first down, it can't play-action on second, and it can't protect on third. That's the whole season in one sentence. The Bombers don't need a new offence. They need to get back to the one that made them a dynasty — under centre, downhill, with a lead blocker and a quarterback throwing off a run fake. Everything else is a workaround for a problem they created.
