Blitz Blueprint: How Winnipeg's Defense Can Dissect Rookie Kaidon Salter

July 30, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

Offensive Identity Chaos: Three QBs, Zero Rhythm

The Bombers' 4-3 record masks a fractured offensive scheme that has cycled through Collaros, Elgersma, and Brown. On the surface, producing victories with three different starters suggests depth, but a deep dive into the play-calling tells a different story. In their Week 6 loss to Calgary, offensive coordinator Buck Pierce leaned on heavy 12 personnel (two backs, two receivers) early, trying to establish Brady Oliveira. On first down, the Bombers ran inside zone from a condensed formation 11 times, gaining 5.2 yards per carry—respectable, but the tendency allowed the Stampeders' linebackers to trigger downhill without hesitation. By the second quarter, Calgary’s defensive line was slanting weakside, blowing up the cutback lanes Oliveira had thrived on in the first half (he finished with 128 yards but 96 came before contact). The predictable run-every-first-down sequence set up constant second-and-longs, forcing backup quarterback Taylor Elgersma into obvious passing downs. He was pressured on 46% of his dropbacks, leading to two turnovers. The film shows a disjointed offense that lacks a cohesive situational identity; until Zack Collaros returns healthy, the Bombers’ game plan must be more unpredictable, using early-down play-action from under center to slow down aggressive box defenders.

Calgary's Blueprint: Exposing Hall's Static 3-4 Front

Defensive coordinator Jordan Hall’s unit gave up 52 points, but the tape reveals a structural problem rather than just poor execution. Hall’s base 3-4 often features a “mugged” look with both inside linebackers crowding the A-gaps pre-snap. Against Calgary, that aggressiveness became a liability. On the Stampeders’ second touchdown drive, quarterback Jake Maier repeatedly attacked the boundary with quick-hitch screens. The mugged backers vacated the flats, leaving cornerback Demerio Houston in one-on-one tackling situations he lost badly—one specific 2nd-and-7 screen gained 34 yards. Calgary also exploited the Bombers’ cover-3 buzz by running deep crossers from the slot, knowing the strong safety cheats down versus run action. The result: three explosive pass plays of 30+ yards. In the second half, Hall adjusted to a split-safety look, but by then Calgary’s tempo had the defense gasping. The costly lesson: without a four-man rush that can win consistently—Willie Jefferson had one pressure on 29 rushes—Hall’s exotic blitzes are leaving vulnerable voids. Winnipeg’s defense is allowing 8.1 yards per attempt on non-blitz plays, a bottom-three mark in the league. Unless Hall integrates more simulated pressures (showing blitz but dropping into coverage), even rookie quarterbacks will dissect the undermanned secondary.

Salter's College Tell: All-or-Nothing Reads and the Rightward Drift

Kaidon Salter’s 2023 tape at Liberty reveals a quarterback who put up gaudy numbers—32 touchdowns, only 6 interceptions, 1,000 rushing yards—but whose decision-making under duress can be manipulated. Film study of his 18 RPO snaps against Oregon last season shows he locks onto the primary slant route immediately, rarely progressing to the backside dig. When that read is covered, Salter defaults to a scramble, nearly always tucking the ball and escaping to his right. This tendency led to 12 sacks, as defenses using a backside defensive end as a “spy” or “robber” cut off his escape lane. Winnipeg can exploit this with a simple wrinkle: align rush end Jefferson wide of the right tackle, then drop him into the right flat as a spy, forcing Salter to keep his eyes in the pocket or risk a deflection. Even more potent would be a “creeping” linebacker from the left side who blitzes off the edge, taking away the quarterback’s first move. Hall can dial up zone blitzes that show a six-man pressure but drop the weakside linebacker into the short middle, where Salter’s tight-window forced throws fall. The rookie’s preseason film shows he struggles against defensive backs showing Cover 2 pre-snap and rotating to Cover 4 post-snap—exactly the disguise Deatrick Nichols and the Bombers’ secondary have used in two-minute situations. If Hall commits to making Salter play from the pocket on third down—a down where the Lions’ offense has converted just 22% this season—the Bombers can produce a dramatic bounce-back performance.

Oliveira’s patient, one-cut style behind pulling guards is devastating against undisciplined gap exchanges.
