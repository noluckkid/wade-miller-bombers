Three Coordinators, One Problem: Inside the Bombers' Offensive Ceiling

November 16, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The 8.7 Percent Mirage

Start with the number that makes the Bombers' offensive "improvement" look better than it is. Winnipeg averaged 25.7 offensive points per game in 2026, up from 21.3 in 2025. That's a 4.4-point jump, and it sounds like a coordinator change that worked. But league-wide scoring climbed 8.7 percent, and that's the adjustment nobody in the building wants to make out loud. Normalize Winnipeg's 25.7 against the league's inflation rate and you get the equivalent of roughly 23.6 points in 2025 conditions, a real gain of about 2.3 points per game, not 4.4. The Bombers gained 2.7 yards per game and 0.3 yards per play. Relative to the league they were standing still, and the standings say so: seventh in points scored, seventh in net yardage, seventh in yards per play. Three different play-callers in three years. Buck Pierce, Jason Hogan, Tommy Condell, producing the same seventh-place output is not a coordinator problem. It's a structural one.

O'Shea knows it. "Because it's three different coordinators, I think you've gotta look at it differently," he told reporters Tuesday. "I don't think I'd be looking at this year and comparing it to last year." That's the most honest thing said about this offence all year. The constant isn't the voice in the headset. It's the quarterback, the protection plan, and a personnel group built to win 24-21 games.

The Sack Ledger: 42 Is a Scheme Number

The one statistical regression that tells you more than the points do is sacks allowed: 42, five more than 2025. Give up more sacks while throwing fewer interceptions. Winnipeg cut giveaways from 45 to 34, and you've described a protection philosophy, not bad luck. Condell's offence, going back to his Hamilton work with Zach Collaros, has always leaned on quick game and run-pass options to keep the quarterback clean. That's the right instinct with a veteran passer. The cost is a route tree that lives between the numbers, a low rate of true deep-developing concepts, and an explosive-play ceiling that caps out around seventh in the league in yards per play.

Watch the tape and the pressure answers are predictable: when a defence brings six, the Bombers' first adjustment is a screen or a hot throw to the back, not a protection slide with a vertical shot behind it. That's survivable against four-man rush teams. Against the CFL's better pressure units, it means second-and-eight becomes second-and-eleven, and Condell's third-down menu shrinks to mesh and rub concepts into tight windows. Winnipeg didn't lose the sack battle because the line got materially worse. They lost it because the ball was held a beat longer on the concepts designed to create explosives, and because 42 sacks is what a quick-game offence surrenders when the quick game stops being quick.

Between the 20s Is Not a Red Zone Plan

Seventh in points, seventh in net yardage, seventh in yards per play is a diagnostic trifecta. When all three align, the problem is systemic, not situational, but the reason the points ranking stings most is field compression. A team that moves the ball with screens, RPOs and option routes has to win in a condensed field where those concepts get defended by eight-man spacing and tighter cushions. Winnipeg's offence generated its yards in the open field and its field goals in the tight one. That's why special teams ranked first and the defence tied for fifth: O'Shea's team was built to win field position and let the other two phases carry the scoreboard. The offence was the swing phase, and it swung seventh.

There's a fair defence of Condell here, and Collaros made it himself at season's end, speaking warmly about the coach he played for in Hamilton. Continuity matters, and O'Shea has signalled Condell is under contract for 2027 and expected back. But continuity with a scheme that tops out at seventh is just a slower version of the carousel.

The East Final and the Weight on the Front Four

The Davis Alexander. David Reese episode is a culture story, and O'Shea handled it the way he handles everything: "I'm not a fan of trash-talking… the toughest guys on the field never really have to speak." But it's also a football story. Alexander's off-script creation is exactly the matchup Winnipeg's front four is built to neutralize, collapse the pocket, keep him in the structure, don't let him extend. When a quarterback who wins on improvisation beats you, the frustration leaks. Reese's alleged comments came in a game where the defence was asked to hold a one-score margin because the offence couldn't separate. That's the real indictment: a first-ranked special-teams unit and a top-five defence spent 2026 covering for a scheme that never gave them breathing room.

What Has to Change in 2027

Three things, and none of them require a new coordinator. First, rebuild the protection plan so the deep-intermediate window exists on early downs, play-action from under centre, not just shotgun RPO. Second, cut the sack number back under 35 by designing hot answers that travel past the sticks. Third, hand the red-zone menu to the personnel: bigger bodies, tighter splits, and a power-run complement that forces defences to defend the goal line honestly. Winnipeg's 34 giveaways and top-ranked special teams prove this roster is disciplined enough to win ugly. What it hasn't proven is that the offence can win a game by itself. Until it can, seventh is the ceiling, and seventh is exactly where the Bombers will finish again.
