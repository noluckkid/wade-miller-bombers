Winnipeg's Offense: More Yards, Fewer Points, Same Problems

November 14, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Coordinator Carousel and the Seventh-Place Scoring Floor

Mike O'Shea told reporters on Tuesday that Winnipeg's lack of scoring is "concerning," but he pushed back on comparing 2026 to 2025 or 2024 because the Blue Bombers have had three different offensive coordinators in three years. The data doesn't let him off the hook. Winnipeg ranked seventh in the CFL in points scored, net yardage, and yards per play in 2026. That is the definition of a league-average offense in a season when league scoring rose 8.7 percent. The Bombers' 25.7 offensive points per game were up from 21.3 in 2025, and they generated 2.7 more yards per game and 0.3 more yards per play. They also cut turnovers from 45 to 34. Those are real improvements. But relative to the league, Winnipeg's offense didn't close the gap. The aggregate rankings make the phase profile clear: offense seventh, defence tied for fifth, special teams first. That is not a team losing because of defence or special teams. Tommy Condell was hired last offseason to replace Jason Hogan, who was demoted back to running backs coach. O'Shea expects Condell back in 2027, and Zach Collaros spoke glowingly about him. Continuity is fine. Continuity without scheme evolution is not.

Film Room: Condell's Horizontal Rhythm vs. Collaros's Pocket

Condell's offense with Collaros is built on rhythm: motion to identify coverage, quick screens and hitches to the boundary, RPO slants, and play-action shots off the run game. Collaros previously played for Condell in Hamilton, so the pre-snap communication is not the issue. The issue is what happens after the snap. Winnipeg's 42 sacks allowed were five more than in 2025, despite a lower turnover count. That combination tells you the offense traded risk for compression. The quick game reduced interceptions, but it also invited second-and-long and third-and-medium. The early-down tendency is telling: Condell uses motion to declare coverage and then throws a quick perimeter screen or hitch. Against off-coverage, that's a five-yard gain. Against press, it's a contested catch. When Winnipeg faced second-and-8 or longer, the film shows Condell reducing the route tree to two-man concepts, which lets opposing pass rushers pin their ears back. That's how you get 42 sacks while reducing turnovers. When you rank seventh in yards per play and seventh in points, the tape usually shows the same pattern: efficient first-down gains that don't create explosive plays, then red-zone possessions that bog down because the field shrinks and Condell's spacing concepts lose their leverage. Collaros is a rhythm passer who needs a clean pocket. The film shows too many reps where the pocket collapses on the second read, forcing checkdowns or sacks. The fix isn't a new coordinator. It's protection structure—chips, max protect on early downs, and more play-action that moves the pocket.

The East Final Flashpoint: Reese, Alexander, and the Defensive Margin

The Blue Bombers' defence was tied for fifth league-wide, and special teams ranked first. That is a phase profile that should win more games. In the East Final against Montreal, Winnipeg's defence kept the game within reach, but the offense couldn't counterpunch. The post-game flashpoint between Davis Alexander and David Reese—Alexander said Reese talked about his late father—is a distraction, but it also underscores the matchup: Montreal's reigning Most Outstanding Player extended plays and forced Winnipeg's rushers into prolonged reps. Reese is a high-motor edge defender; when the rush lane breaks down, that motor turns into late contact and chatter. O'Shea said he's not a fan of trash-talking and wouldn't condone it. The X's and O's takeaway is more important: Winnipeg's defence is good enough to carry a middling offense only if the offense converts short fields into touchdowns. It didn't. Alexander's scramble ability broke Winnipeg's edge contain, and the Bombers' offense never made Montreal pay with a counterpunch drive. That is the margin in a one-score East Final.

The Fix: Red-Zone Design, Protection, and a Vertical Threat

O'Shea's "three coordinators" defense is coach-speak. The same core players have produced the same scoring ceiling. If Condell returns in 2027, the Blue Bombers need a red-zone package that creates conflict for second-level defenders: rub routes, bootleg flood concepts, and play-action tight-end leaks. They need a vertical threat to back off safeties on first down. They need a protection plan that keeps Collaros upright—42 sacks is too many for a quarterback who wins with timing. The special teams and defence bought Winnipeg margin in 2026. The offense spent it. Until Condell's scheme produces points instead of yards, O'Shea's concern will be the defining story of the Blue Bombers' offseason.
