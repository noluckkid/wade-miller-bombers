Bombers Film Room: Why Winnipeg Is Winning Ugly Again

October 14, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

Twelve Personnel, Gap Runs and the Collaros Play-Action Ladder

Winnipeg's Week 19 win over a West Division rival was not a showcase of modern spread football. It was a 1990s-style beatdown wearing 2026 clothing, and the film explains why it worked. Offensive coordinator Jason Hogan has quietly pushed the Bombers back toward 12-personnel — two tight ends, one back, two receivers — on roughly a third of early-down snaps, and the payoff is a run game that no longer asks Brady Oliveira to win alone.

Watch the tape and the same blocking picture repeats: a tight end or H-back kicking out the edge, a pulling guard on a G-lead, Oliveira pressing the A-gap before bouncing to the alley. That is gap-scheme football, not the zone-heavy menu Winnipeg ran through Buck Pierce's final seasons. The distinction matters. Gap runs force linebackers to read and fill rather than run and chase, and against Saskatchewan's and Edmonton's second-level defenders, that hesitation has been worth a half-yard per carry — the difference between second-and-6 and second-and-4, which is the difference between a punt and a fresh set of downs.

The run game is the setup, not the punchline. Zach Collaros's play-action numbers have climbed precisely because defenses now have to honor the dive. Hogan has been stacking dagger-style concepts — a deep dig behind a seam-clearing post — off the same backfield action, and Collaros is delivering with his feet set. When the pocket is clean, he remains one of the three most accurate intermediate passers in the CFL. When it isn't, the offence has a tell: the quick bubble to Nic Demski on the back side, which has effectively become Winnipeg's punt on second-and-long.

That is the vulnerability. Winnipeg's pressure-to-sack rate on obvious passing downs sits near the bottom of the league, and the escape hatch is a screen or checkdown rather than a shot. Kenny Lawler's contested-catch ability masks it in the red zone, where the Bombers lean on back-shoulder fades to the boundary and let him win 50-50 balls. Between the 20s, though, this offence is content to win in four-yard increments and hand the game to its defence and kicking teams.

Jordan Younger's Amoeba Fronts and the Boundary Bait

Defensive coordinator Jordan Younger has built something genuinely difficult to prepare for. Winnipeg's base is a four-man front, but the pre-snap picture is deliberately ambiguous: Willie Jefferson stands up as a wide-9 on one snap, drops into the hook on the next, and crashes the C-gap on the third. The 34-year-old isn't a every-down edge rusher anymore — his snap count has been managed — but he remains the league's best decoy, because offensive tackles cannot set their feet until they know whether he's coming.

The real damage comes from the second level. Younger has leaned hard on simulated pressures: five defenders mugging the A-gaps, two dropping out, and a coverage shell that rotates late from two-high to single-high. Against young quarterbacks, that rotation is a trap. The Bombers have baited at least four interceptions this season by showing Cover 2 and rolling to a boundary trap with Deatrick Nichols sitting on the out-cut. It is not exotic. It is disciplined, and it is repeated until the quarterback proves he can beat it.

The run defence tells a similar story. Winnipeg plays a heavy dose of "match" quarters behind a penetrating front, which puts linebackers in conflict but keeps safeties free to fill. The trade-off is explosive runs when the front gets displaced — the Bombers have surrendered more 20-plus-yard carries than O'Shea would like — but the red-zone numbers justify it. Inside the 20, Younger tightens to man-free and dares teams to throw fades against Nichols and Evan Holm, and the results have been excellent.

Third-and-Short, Sergio Castillo and the O'Shea Doctrine

Mike O'Shea's game management has become a genuine schematic weapon. Winnipeg goes for it on third-and-2 or less from anywhere past its own 40, and the short-yardage package is a tell-free formation: Oliveira offset, a tight end in motion, Collaros under center with the option to sneak or hand off. Defenses have to defend three things from one look, and the Bombers convert at a rate that makes punting look like the conservative play.

Sergio Castillo's leg has quietly stabilized a kicking game that cost Winnipeg games in 2024. His accuracy inside 40 yards has been near-automatic, and Jamieson Sheahan's directional punting has flipped field position in the fourth quarter of three separate wins. In a West Division race likely decided by one game, those hidden yards are the margin.

What the West Race Actually Demands

The path forward is a matchup problem. B.C.'s defensive front — five sacks in its most recent outing — will test Winnipeg's interior protection in a way Saskatchewan could not, and Nathan Rourke's ability to extend plays stresses the discipline of Younger's second-level defenders. Edmonton's Malik Carney, coming off a four-sack performance, is exactly the kind of speed rusher who punishes a 12-personnel offence on obvious passing downs.

Winnipeg does not need to become explosive. It needs to stay on schedule, protect the football, and force opponents to drive 90 yards against a defence that thrives on baiting mistakes. That formula is unglamorous, it is aging, and through Week 19 it is still working. In a division where Montreal looks borderline unbeatable and B.C. has won four straight, "still working" may be enough to host a playoff game — and in Winnipeg, that has always been the standard.
