Bombers' 33-21 Win Over Ottawa: Film Shows Red-Zone Cracks Underneath

August 16, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

Friday Night's Win: The Spark That Still Needs a Fire

Let's start with the numbers that matter: the Winnipeg Blue Bombers scored 33 points on Friday night at Princess Auto Stadium before 31,312, snapped a two-game losing streak and moved to 5-4 on the season. But the 33-21 final against the still-winless Ottawa RedBlacks (0-9) was not a statement win, despite Nic Demski's 79 yards on six catches and Zach Collaros' return under centre. The film reveals an offence that still sputters in the red zone and a defence that relies on turnovers, not control. The next four games are Edmonton (6-3), Montreal, then the Labour Day Classic and the Banjo Bowl against Saskatchewan. That is not a stretch for moral victories; that is a crucible.

The Collaros Effect: Play-Action Returns to the Menu

Zach Collaros' return gave the Blue Bombers' playbook its missing layer. With him at quarterback, the offence can use hard play-action to pull Ottawa's safety down and deliver fast, intermediate throws to the seams. Demski's 79-yard night was the direct beneficiary: the RedBlacks' secondary sat in a two-shell for most of the first half, allowing Collaros to hit Demski on over routes and deep digs between the hashes. That is the "spark" the team referenced after the game — not just the quarterback's presence, but the freedom to call 12 personnel run-action off play fakes, a tendency that disappeared during the two-game home losing streak.

But here is the nuance the coaches will see on the film: Ottawa, which has lost 15 consecutive games dating back to 2025, still generated pressure with interior stunts, forcing Collaros to climb the pocket on several third downs. The Bombers' tackles handled the edges, but the guards struggled against delay blitzes. It worked against an 0-9 team because no coverage could hold up for 4.5 seconds. That luxury evaporates against Montreal. The Alouettes will play single-high safety, show blitz, then drop into zone — and if Collaros's internal clock is affected by an early pressure, the quick-game timing that produced Demski's 79 yards will be the first thing to warp.

David Reese's Interception: A Scheme Win, Not a Free Play

David Reese's first career interception was the defensive highlight, and it wasn't a broken-play ball. The Bombers showed a five-man pressure look from the boundary, then dropped Reese into a shallow hook zone on the backside of a "green dog" — a blitz simulation that lets the rusher become a coverage defender if the pass comes out fast. Ottawa's quarterback read pressure and tried to hit a curl at the sticks; Reese jumped the route and made a play that a disciplined CFL defence can manufacture even when its pass rush fails to get home. That is good scheme design.

The concerning companion is the 21 points allowed to a team that entered the night with the league's longest active losing streak. Winnipeg's base defence is built on a soft Cover 3 with nickel depth, designed to keep everything in front and rally to the ball. It works on second-and-long, but the RedBlacks converted too many second-and-short runs into manageable third downs. The defensive tackles were blocked into down blocks, leaving the Mike linebacker to scrape and fill — a reactive posture that allowed Ottawa to extend drives. Against Montreal, whose receivers will test the outside third with real speed, that same coverage shell gets stretched vertically. The Bombers' secondary will have to play tighter outside leverage or bring an extra safety into the box. They didn't need to against Ottawa. They will need to next week.

Red-Zone Stagnation: The Brady Oliveira Warning

The most telling numbers from Friday are not 33-21. They are the field goals that capped drives that reached the Ottawa red zone. Brady Oliveira said it explicitly: "We scored 33... there's times I felt we should've finished drives but you've got to score points in this league." Watch the film and you see a pattern: on first-and-goal from inside the ten, the Bombers called under-centre dive runs to Oliveira, packing the formation with two tight ends. The RedBlacks' defensive front lined up in a bear front, sacrificing a contain defender to put eight in the box. It worked; Oliveira was met at the line by unblocked run-throughs. The play-action pass, a weapon that had been so effective earlier in the drive, was abandoned once the field compressed.

That tendency is a goldmine for upcoming opponents. The Edmonton Elks, who possess what Oliveira called a "great defence" in his post-game comments, will stack the box and force Collaros to throw from compressed looks. Montreal will bring double-mug blitzes on running downs and trust their cornerbacks in man coverage. Saskatchewan, with its Labour Day and Banjo Bowl history, will likewise load the line of scrimmage. If the play-calling doesn't diversify — if the response to a bear front continues to be an inside run rather than a play-action boot or a jet sweep to Demski — the Bombers will turn wins like Friday into a 1-3 stretch in these next four games.

The Verdict: A Win to Build On, Not a Standard to Trust

No one is suggesting the Bombers should apologize for their fifth win of 2026. The result ends a worrying home-losing streak and keeps Winnipeg in third place in the West Division. But the fundamental questions remain: can this offensive line, which struggled against Ottawa's interior stunts, handle Edmonton's pressure package? Can this secondary, which gave up 21 to an 0-9 offence, sustain its coverage against Montreal's explosive skill groups? And can a red-zone attack that stalls when the field shrinks score the majors needed to beat a defending Grey Cup champion in back-to-back weeks?

The answers will determine whether 5-4 becomes 6-6 or 8-4. Right now, the film says the Bombers are a team that beat a winless opponent by twelve despite not playing clean.
