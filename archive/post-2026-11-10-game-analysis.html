The Bombers Didn't Lose the East Final on Talent — They Lost on Tendencies

November 10, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Protection Tell: What Montreal Saw on Tape

Winnipeg's protection is a half-slide with the back scanning to the front side. Against static four-man fronts, it works. Against movement — T/E twists, delayed A-gap loops, walked-up second-level defenders — it asks the guard and center to re-anchor in a phone booth while the tackle handles a wide-9 that's already two steps upfield.

Montreal attacked that all afternoon. The Alouettes didn't need exotic pressures. They ran stunts off the edge and let interior rushers work back against the grain of the slide, and the Bombers' interior lost the re-anchor battle repeatedly. Stanley Bryant turns 39 this spring and Patrick Neufeld turns 38 next month — after nearly 400 combined games in blue and gold. The tape doesn't lie about what that means: the hands are still right, the feet are a half-beat slow, and a half-beat is the whole game against a front that rushes with intent.

This is where the East Final turned. Not on a deep shot, not on a turnover — on second-and-long snaps where Zach Collaros had to hitch at the top of his drop because the pocket collapsed from the middle, not the edge. Collaros has always been a rhythm thrower who wins with anticipation on in-breaking routes. Take away his platform and you've taken away the entire offense's reason for existing.

Third-and-Short Is a Doctrine, Not a Scheme

The Bombers' short-yardage identity is Brady Oliveira downhill behind a heavy package, with Collaros under center and a fullback or an extra tight end as a lead. It's been the league's most reliable one-yard play for three seasons. It's also been the most predictable one.

What Montreal did was simple and devastating: they loaded the A-gaps and crashed the edges, betting Winnipeg wouldn't throw out of the formation. And they were right. There's no bootleg off the short-yardage look, no leak-out to the tight end, no quick slant behind the crashing end. When the Bombers did stay on the field on second-and-two, the call sheet tightened rather than expanded — run, run, and if it fails, punt from your own side and hand a short field to a Montreal offense that only needs three downs to cash it.

The play-calling tendency that shows up hardest on film is the disappearance of play-action on early downs. Winnipeg's most efficient concept all year was boot-action off outside zone with the tight end dragging across the formation. In the East Final, that concept showed up almost exclusively on second down — which is the one down where a smart defensive coordinator is looking for it. Montreal's linebackers never bit. They sat, they read the pullers, and they tackled the check-down for four yards.

Jordan Younger's Four-Man Rush Is Running Out of Bodies

Jordan Younger's defense is built on a principle: rush four, play coverage, and trust Willie Jefferson and the edge rotation to win without help. It's a great principle when you have three edge rushers who can collapse a pocket. It's a crisis when the rotation is 35-year-old Jefferson, Kydran Jenkins, Matt Jaworski, and a lot of hope.

The film shows the cost. Against Montreal, the Bombers' linebackers — Jovan Santos-Knox, Kyrie Wilson, Michael Ayers, Jaiden Woodbey — were asked to handle both the run fit and the intermediate middle, and the Alouettes attacked that conflict with play-action crossers and delayed releases from the backfield. When Winnipeg finally brought a fifth rusher, the coverage behind it was a spot-drop zone that Montreal's quarterback picked apart at the seams. When they rushed four, they couldn't get home.

That's not a talent problem. That's a numbers problem, and it's about to get worse.

27 Names, One Scheme, Zero Continuity

Count the list. Nineteen players on the active roster hit the open market in February, plus eight practice-roster players who are already free. Of those 27, fifteen are defensive players — including both starting outside linebackers, three-quarters of the secondary rotation, and Jefferson himself. Four are offensive linemen, plus a Global tackle on the practice roster.

Deatrick Nichols and Nick Hallett at the back end. Wilson, Santos-Knox, Ayers, Woodbey, Tony Jones at the second level. Thiadric Hansen and Tanner Schmekel on teams. That's not a free-agency class. That's a depth chart.

And the two most important names aren't a scheme question at all. If Neufeld and Bryant both retire — and Neufeld's own words about "the toll this game takes" and three young kids say he's genuinely undecided for the first time in his career — Winnipeg is replacing both guards, or its left tackle of the last decade, in one offseason. Gabe Wallace and Micah Vanterpool are the internal answers, and the tape on both says "functional starter," not "anchor."

The Bombers' scheme didn't fail because it got old. It failed because the scheme was always asking the oldest, most expensive, least replaceable players on the roster to absorb the hardest assignments, and this year the bill came due. Whoever is under center next season — Collaros, if he comes back; a bridge starter, if he doesn't — inherits a protection call that needs a redesign, a short-yardage package that needs a counter, and a pass rush that needs a fifth body.

Thirty-three to twenty-three wasn't a talent gap. It was a tendency report. And the whole league has a copy.
