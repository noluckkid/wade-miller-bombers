Condell's Ceiling: Why Winnipeg's Offence Keeps Landing Seventh

November 12, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Seventh-Place Ceiling

Start with the arithmetic Mike O'Shea would rather you not do. Winnipeg's offence averaged 25.7 points per game in 2026, up from 21.3. It gained 2.7 more yards per game and 0.3 more yards per play. Turnovers dropped from 45 to 34. By any year-over-year read, Tommy Condell's first season was an improvement. Then you get to the punchline: seventh in points scored, seventh in net yardage, seventh in yards per play. League-wide scoring climbed 8.7 percent. The Bombers ran hard to stay in the same place.

O'Shea pushed back on the comparison game Tuesday — "because it's three different coordinators, I think you've gotta look at it differently" — and he's half right. Buck Pierce left for B.C., Jason Hogan got one year and a demotion back to running backs coach, Condell inherited the room in 2026. Three playbooks, one ranking. When the variable changes three times and the output doesn't move, you stop blaming the variable. The constant is the personnel, the quarterback's age curve, and an offensive line that just surrendered 42 sacks, five more than a year ago.

Uniform Mediocrity Is a Scheme Problem, Not Bad Luck

Here's the tell in the aggregate numbers, and it's the most important thing on the page: Winnipeg is seventh in points, seventh in yardage, and seventh in yards per play. Those three rankings being identical matters. A team that moves the ball but can't finish is a red-zone problem — fixable with a handful of concepts. A team that finishes but can't move it is an explosive-play problem — fixable with tempo and shot selection. The Bombers are neither. They are the same team on first down, between the twenties, and in the scoring area. That's a systemic ceiling, not a leaky phase.

What it looks like on tape is an offence that lives in the intermediate middle and dies there. Condell's Hamilton DNA — vertical spacing, four-vertical stress, deeper quarterback drops — asks the receiver room to win at the top of routes against press and asks the line to hold long enough for those routes to develop. Winnipeg's roster is built for the opposite: play-action off an interior run game, quick game, catch-and-run. The marriage produced a lot of second-and-seven. Second-and-seven is survivable in this league. It is not how you get to 30 points in a shootout.

Forty-Two Sacks and the Protection Arithmetic

Sacks are the most misattributed stat in football. Fans blame the line; coordinators blame the quarterback; the truth is usually structural. Winnipeg gave up five more sacks in 2026 while throwing fewer interceptions and scoring more — which tells you the ball was being held, not forced. Condell's offence leans on defined drops and full-field progression reads, and Collaros, at this stage, is at his best when the ball comes out on rhythm to a pre-snap answer. Every concept that requires him to reset and scan a second window is a coin flip behind a front that isn't generating movement in the run game the way it did in the championship years.

The fix isn't philosophical, it's mechanical: more six-man protections with a back scanning inside-out, more moving pockets, more bootleg off the run action that made Collaros a Most Outstanding Player. Those are chip-and-slide answers that cost you a route and buy you a half-second. Winnipeg's staff chose to protect with scheme spacing instead. The 42 says that trade lost.

The Turnover Dividend Has a Hidden Invoice

Eleven fewer giveaways is real value — roughly a possession every game and a half of field position and expected points — and it's the single best argument for Condell's return. But it isn't free. Clean football at this volume usually comes from shorter throws, covered checkdowns, and a quarterback who has been coached into risk aversion. Pair that with the sack spike and you get the profile of an offence that is choosing the safe negative over the aggressive positive. In a league scoring 8.7 percent more, safe is a losing strategy. You cannot out-execute a rising tide.

Defence and Special Teams Are Paying the Mortgage

O'Shea's team won games in 2026 because the other two phases covered the invoice. The defence tied for fifth league-wide; special teams ranked first. That first-place special-teams ranking is doing more work than the box score shows — hidden yards, starting field position, and the short fields that turn a 25.7-point offence into a winning record. Strip out the special-teams edge and Winnipeg has to score 28-plus to survive, and nothing in the 2026 film suggests it can do that against a top-four defence on demand. That's the real risk of running it back: you're betting a first-place unit repeats.

The Discipline Tax and the Reese Fallout

O'Shea was candid Tuesday about David Reese's alleged taunting of Davis Alexander regarding his late father: he's "not a fan of trash-talking," doesn't "want to take penalties because of it," and conceded "you're not going to stop it." Fair. But the coach's own framing gives the analytics away. On a team whose margin is built on hidden yards, a rotational defensive lineman trading 15 yards of objectionable conduct for a verbal jab is a straight-up bad trade — and the East Final environment it created is not a coincidence. Discipline is a scheme decision when your offence can't absorb the field-position swing.

What Year Two Has to Fix

O'Shea expects Condell back, and he's under contract through 2027. That continuity is genuinely new — Collaros hasn't had the same coordinator in consecutive seasons since Pierce — and Collaros's season-ending endorsement of Condell is the strongest internal signal in the building.
