Dru Brown Won the Game. The Tape Says Winnipeg Has a Bigger Problem.

November 04, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

An 18-of-30 That Flatters Nobody

Winnipeg beat Toronto 25-22 on Saturday in front of 21,786 at BMO Field, and the box score reads like competent caretaking: Dru Brown, 18-of-30, 206 yards, two touchdowns, two interceptions. That's 6.9 yards per attempt — a number that sits below the efficiency line for a CFL starter in a league that hands out yards in chunks. But the raw line actually overstates how well he played, because both of his best moments were manufactured by somebody else.

The first-quarter touchdown to Ontaria Wilson was a drag route against what looked like a match-zone look with the flat defender widening. Brown had pressure in his lap, threw off his back foot, and the ball nearly sailed past Wilson's reach — a one-handed grab, then 32 yards of YAC to the end zone. That's a completion created by a receiver's catch radius and a defender's poor pursuit angle, not by a read. Flip it: if Wilson doesn't make that play, the ball is a free interception to a trailing linebacker. Same throw, opposite headline.

Then the two picks. The DaShaun Amos interception wasn't on Brown — Dorian Singer ran a deep curl, lost his footing coming back to the ball, and the pass hit Amos in the chest with nothing but green in front of him. But the second one, late in the second quarter, was a decision: a forced ball into a double coverage shell that Dathan Hickey read from the jump. That's the throw that tells you where Brown is mentally. Under duress and with the pocket tightening, he reverted to his pre-snap answer instead of working to the second concept.

Buck Pierce Is Calling a Shorter Game Sheet Than He Wants To

Watch the sequence of Winnipeg's first downs and you see an offensive coordinator protecting his quarterback's read burden. Buck Pierce has leaned hard into 11 personnel, Brady Oliveira downhill on first down, and play-action on second-and-short — the classic way to hand a backup a two-read progression and a clean pocket. The screen game and the quick perimeter stuff aren't window dressing; they're substitutes for the intermediate middle of the field, where Brown's eyes get sticky.

The most encouraging call of the day was the 23-yard game-winner to Nic Demski late in the fourth: a slot concept that used traffic to spring him, giving Brown a defined, on-time throw with a receiver in space. Note the sequencing — Pierce got to that concept after 55 minutes of asking Brown to do it the hard way. When the field compresses and the spacing concepts lose their cushion, Winnipeg's red-zone offense starts bleeding into field goals. That's a structural problem: this is a heavy lateral, spacing-based passing game that needs a quarterback willing to attack the seams and the back-shoulder throws at the goal line. Brown hasn't shown that yet, and Pierce's call sheet reflects it.

What Noel Thorpe Will Steal From This Tape

Now the Bombers go to Percival Molson on Saturday, Nov. 7 at 3:00 p.m. ET to face a 14-4 Alouettes team that already beat them once at home — the two regular-season meetings were split, each team defending its own building. Noel Thorpe's defense is the exact wrong matchup for a quarterback who needs clean reads to survive. Thorpe pressures more than almost anyone in the league, and he disguises it better: second-level defenders walked up, safeties rotating late, and Marc-Antoine Dequoy sitting as the eraser over the top. Thorpe will have watched the Hickey interception four times.

Expect the bracket on Demski. Montreal's SAM and halfback will double him in the slot with Dequoy rolling down over the top, which drags Brown's progression to the boundary — exactly where he showed he'll force the ball. Winnipeg's counter has to be 12 personnel with Oliveira as the check-down valve, max protection to buy Brown time against Thorpe's five-man rushes, and motion to force Montreal to declare their coverage pre-snap. The Bombers also need to win the field-position battle: the kicking game will be a factor in a cold, windy, late-season game in Montreal, and Sergio Castillo's leg has to be a weapon rather than a hedge.

Defensively, Jordan Younger's group has to slow the Alouettes' rhythm concepts without exposing a man-heavy secondary that has lived on Deatrick Nichols' instincts at halfback. The Bombers' pass rush needs to get home on first down, because Montreal's offense is a different animal when it's playing ahead of the chains.

Billy Edwards Jr. Is a Verdict, Not a Roster Move

On Tuesday the Bombers added quarterback Billy Edwards Jr. to their 45-man negotiation list. That's not the news — it's a signal. Edwards is a 6-foot-3, 228-pound 23-year-old who's thrown for 1,872 yards, 15 touchdowns and five interceptions at 63.4 percent over eight starts at North Carolina this season, with a 4-4 record. The résumé has real texture: a Music City Bowl MVP at Maryland after beating Auburn 31-13, a 3,322-yard, 19-touchdown, 10-interception three-year run in College Park, and a lost 2025 at Wisconsin marred by a knee injury and a 43.8 percent completion rate.

Why claim him now? Because Zach Collaros is 38 and coming off a rib injury that pushed Brown into the biggest start of his career, and because Brown is set to hit free agency after a performance that created more questions than answers. Adding a negotiation-list name doesn't mean the player knows or cares — it costs nothing and blocks 44 other teams from a lottery ticket. But it's an admission that the Bombers aren't certain the answer is in the room.

The tape from Saturday says the same thing. Winnipeg is 10-9 with a quarterback who was good enough to win one round and a scheme that's currently built to hide him. Montreal is 14-4, at home, with a coordinator who will spend the week attacking exactly the reads Brown struggled with. If Pierce opens up the middle of the field and trusts his quarterback, the Bombers have a chance.
