Pavia's UFL Detour Exposes Winnipeg's Real Problem: A 2022 Playbook

November 19, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Neg List as Confession

Kyle Walters has held Diego Pavia's exclusive CFL rights since June 1, 2024, the second-longest tenure on Winnipeg's negotiation list behind two-time FCS champion Mark Gronowski. He had preliminary conversations with Pavia's reps in April and said the club was monitoring his trajectory. This week, Pavia went first overall to the Columbus Aviators in the 2027 UFL Draft, formally closing a door that, by all appearances, was never more than cracked.

Here is the part that matters more than the transaction: teams do not chase five-foot-ten, 207-pound dual-threat quarterbacks unless the offensive staff believes it is missing a constraint player. Pavia's Vanderbilt résumé, 65.7 percent completions, 5,832 yards, 49 touchdowns against 12 interceptions, plus 1,663 rushing yards and 18 more scores, 17-9 as a starter, two All-SEC nods and a Heisman finalist invite, is the profile of an RPO/split-zone operator. Quick game married to designed runs, backside end reads, the quarterback as a second puller in the run game. That is not Winnipeg's offense. Winnipeg's offense is dropback-and-diagnose with Zach Collaros and wide-zone play-action off Brady Oliveira. Pursuing Pavia was an implicit acknowledgment that the dropback version has a ceiling, and the league found it.

First Down Is the Whole Ballgame

Chart the Bombers' early downs and the tendency is stubborn to the point of self-harm. Heavy 11 personnel, Collaros under centre, Oliveira on inside zone and pin-pull, and a philosophical commitment to running into loaded boxes to "stay on schedule." Against a six-man box, that is fine. Against the seven- and eight-man fronts Winnipeg saw in the second half of 2026, it is a wasted down, and it manufactures the exact second-and-eight that lets a defensive coordinator call whatever he wants.

The CFL field is 65 yards wide with hash marks nine yards off each sideline, which means the wide side of the field is roughly 47 yards of real estate. It is the league's great structural advantage, and Winnipeg underuses it on first down. The Bombers' boundary runs get swallowed because the corner and Sam can set the edge with no threat of a field-side counter. Flip the strength, force the defence to declare its leverage, and the same Oliveira carry becomes a five-yard gain with a cutback lane. That is not a talent problem. It is a formation and play-calling problem, and it is fixable in a meeting room.

Play-action tells the same story. Winnipeg's play-action dropbacks have consistently outpaced its straight-dropback efficiency, the intermediate windows open because the second level bites on Oliveira, yet the frequency stays conservative. The offense is best when it lies to the defence and worst when it plays honest.

Two-High Shells and the Death of the Deep Cross

Collaros's signature throw remains the deep crosser and dig behind the linebackers against single-high coverage. Defensive coordinators responded the way they always do: split-safety shells, palms and cover-two match, with the field safety capping the No. 2 vertical and the Mike walling the intermediate crosser. That closes the 18-to-22-yard window that made Collaros an MOP, and it funnels him to the checkdown.

Watch the tape and you see the same sequence on repeat: the deep concept gets two-high'd, the pocket holds, and the ball goes to Oliveira on a four-yard swing on first-and-10. Nic Demski and Dalton Schoen are reliable, but they are not 6'3" contested-catch winners, and asking them to work the violent middle of the field on a compressed clock is how you get a 12-game injury report. The answer is not a new quarterback. It is motion, bunch and stack releases to beat press, and a genuine vertical threat at the X that forces the safety to honor depth.

The Interior Five and the Four-Man Rush

None of the above survives bad pass protection. Winnipeg's interior has been a rotating cast at centre and right guard, and the tell is how often the Bombers need a running back or tight end to chip. In the 2025 West Final against Saskatchewan, the Roughriders generated pressure with four and dropped eight. Malik Carney off the edge, the interior collapsing the pocket, and Winnipeg had no answer because there was no hot throw built into the concept. When you need six to block four, you have three receivers running into seven defenders.

The division rivals show the contrast. Saskatchewan just re-signed four Canadians, including left guard Erik Andersen, a 6'6", 314-pound second-rounder who made three starts in 2026, and defensive lineman Liam Hoskins, who dressed 11 games with a sack and a forced fumble. That is ratio depth used to protect American skill talent. Winnipeg's Canadian core is older, thinner, and increasingly asked to hold up in protection rather than win at the point of attack.

What the Pavia Blueprint Would Actually Fix

A legitimate running threat at quarterback changes a defence's arithmetic. The backside end cannot crash on the zone read. The Sam cannot scrape unchecked. Twelve personnel becomes a real constraint rather than a short-yardage novelty. But the Bombers do not need Pavia to install quarterback-designed runs and RPOs, they need the willingness to install them, and a coordinator empowered to break tendency. Until then, Winnipeg will keep playing a 2022 playbook against 2026 coverages, and the negotiation list will keep reading like a wish list.
