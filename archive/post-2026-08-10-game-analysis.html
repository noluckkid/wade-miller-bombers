Film Study: The Bye Can’t Hide the Bombers’ Run-Defense Rots

August 10, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The July 5th Ripple Effect

The Winnipeg Blue Bombers have spent the last ten days explaining not just one loss, but the structural flaws that turned a 4-2 start into a 4-4 slog. On July 5th, Zach Collaros was drilled by Reggie Stubblefield in a win over Hamilton. He missed the next four games. Dru Brown won in Toronto and Ottawa, then got exposed in losses to Calgary and B.C. — the worst of those being a 35-19 defeat in which the Lions ran for an astonishing 377 yards. The convenient narrative is that Collaros’ return in Week 9 will stabilize the entire operation. That is only half true. The film suggests the quarterback was never the root cause of the defensive collapse. The problem is structural, and it predates his injury.

What B.C. Showed: Run-Fit Failures, Not Just Missed Tackles

Go back to the fourth quarter of the B.C. game. Winnipeg’s defense had already surrendered 200-plus yards on the ground, but the scheme kept presenting the same soft front. The Lions ran a steady diet of inside zone out of 12 personnel, forcing the Bombers into base nickel. When Winnipeg overloaded the strong side, B.C. simply ran the other direction — cutting off the backside defensive end’s angles and asking the middle linebacker to make plays in space that he was not positioned to make. 377 rushing yards is not a missed-tackle problem. It is a gap-integrity failure. Defensive ends repeated too far upfield. Linebackers scraped late. Safeties crept down late and then lost run-pass conflict. The bye-week return of defensive tackle Cam Lawson helps with one plug in the middle, but Lawson alone does not fix the defensive front’s tendency to get pinned by combo blocks.

The sharper concern is the lack of identifiable adjustment. Winnipeg has used a heavy rotation along the defensive line, but opponents have learned that lateral movement in the run game is the weakness. Calgary and B.C. both attacked the edges with pullers and jet-motion handoffs that pulled the backer away from the point of attack. The Bombers’ back seven is playing too reactive, and there is no one on tape who consistently sets a hard edge against tight formations. That has to change before Collaros’ return matters — because if the defense cannot get off the field, even a healthy quarterback cannot climb out of double-digit deficits.

Brady Oliveira and the Diminishing Return of 100 Yards

Offensively, the Bombers are leaning into a workload that has stopped paying off in the win column. Brady Oliveira leads the CFL in carries and rushing yards, and his three rushing touchdowns match his totals from each of the last two seasons. He is on pace for 45 receptions, down from a career-best 61 a year ago. Over his last two games — both losses? Actually, the split is 24 carries for 176 yards in the previous two contests, and 33 carries for 235 yards in the two losses to Calgary and B.C. That is 7.1 yards per carry in the recent stretch, but it is being spent in games where the defense is bleeding. The usage is the issue: Winnipeg is leaning into Oliveira like he is a 2019-era workhorse, but the play-action pass that used to make those runs so efficient is not threatening defenses.

Historic context is damning. From 2021-24, Oliveira had 17 100-yard rushing games, and the Bombers went 16-1 in those contests. Over the last two seasons, he has seven 100-yard performances — and the team is 3-4 in those games. The difference is the explosive pass game. In 2021-22, those runs set up deep shots off fake dives; now, defenses press the box and make the 100-yard game a punishment, not a weapon. The film shows opposing safeties staying in the post and forcing Collaros or Brown to win underneath. With Brown, the offense became six-yard curls and check-downs to running backs. Collaros’ return changes the calculus. He is a top-tier play-action passer, and the Bombers need to use him that way. Expect more motion, more bootlegs, and more shots to intermediate seams over the top of the stretched defense.

The Pass Rush Is Real, but the Coverage Is Static

The one area that has improved, at least on paper, is the pass rush. Winnipeg has 17 sacks through eight games — tied with Saskatchewan for second, one behind Calgary’s 18. Willie Jefferson and Jake Ceresna each have six sacks, tied with B.C.’s Mathieu Betts for the league lead. This comes after a 2025 season in which the Bombers managed just 23 sacks and finished last in the CFL. Pro Football Focus numbers have the pressure rate climbing steadily, and Jefferson looks closer to the disruptive force he was during the 2021 dynasty run. But the film shows an uncomfortable truth: those pressures are not forcing turnovers because the coverage is soft and the run defense keeps opponents ahead of schedule. When an opponent is in second-and-four, they can run again, and the sack numbers become cosmetic. Jefferson gets home on long passing downs; the Bombers are not creating those downs enough because of the run failures.

This sets up the critical matchup to watch when Collaros returns. Winnipeg’s offensive tackles — including right tackle Jarell Broxton, back from injury — will have to handle elite edge pressure so the offense can sustain drives. The defense, meanwhile, needs high-effort defenders to stay disciplined even when Jefferson cuts loose upfield. The talent is there. The execution is not.

What the Second Bye Must Fix

This became a two-week setup for the second half of the schedule. Collaros’ return is the obvious headline, and he has every reason to be confident: since 2019, he has thrived after injury stretches, and he has a cast he trusts. But the Bombers cannot simply outscore teams. The defense must return to its old identity of forcing two-and-outs by winning early downs. That means ending the mental-bistake issues in the front seven, not overloading the box, and trusting the backside pursuit. The B.C.
