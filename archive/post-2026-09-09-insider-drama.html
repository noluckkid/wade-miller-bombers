Inside the Bombers' Fractured Dynasty: Loyalty, Contracts, and a Crumbling Core

September 09, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Cap Trap: Paying for 2021 in 2025

Zach Collaros is 36 years old, and he's playing like it. Through the first half of the 2025 season, his completion percentage has dipped below 65 percent, his yards per attempt have cratered, and he's absorbing sacks at a rate that would have been unthinkable during the dynasty years. Yet the Blue Bombers have committed roughly $600,000 of their $5.5 million salary cap to him — over 10 percent of the entire cap — for a quarterback who no longer elevates the offence. The contract extension he signed in 2024 was framed as a reward for two Grey Cup rings. In reality, it was a cap anchor that has handcuffed general manager Kyle Walters from addressing the roster's most glaring weaknesses.

The problem isn't just Collaros. It's the entire championship core. Willie Jefferson, 34, is earning elite defensive end money while his sack production has declined for three straight seasons — from 11 in 2022 to 7 in 2023 to 5 in 2024. Adam Bighill, 36, is being paid like a first-team All-Star at a position where speed and lateral movement are everything; his range in coverage has visibly shrunk. Stanley Bryant, 39, is the oldest offensive lineman in the league, and the pressure Collaros faces every snap tells the story. These aren't bad players — they're good players being paid like great ones, and the cap math doesn't lie. When the Bombers lost to Saskatchewan 32-26 on Labour Day, the offensive line surrendered four sacks and countless pressures against a Riders front that had been mediocre all season.

The Locker Room Divide: Veterans vs. The Next Generation

Behind the scenes, the tension is palpable. Multiple sources within the organization describe a locker room split between the "championship core" — the veterans who won in 2019 and 2021 — and the younger players who feel they're carrying the load without the recognition or the paycheques. When Brady Oliveira signed his two-year extension worth $210,000 per season, it was spun as a hometown discount. But Oliveira is 28, in his prime, and leading the league in rushing. He's watching 36-year-olds make three times his salary while he carries the offence. The message it sends to the rest of the roster is corrosive: loyalty is rewarded over production.

The receiver room has been a particular flashpoint. Dalton Schoen's devastating knee injury in 2024 left a void that the front office failed to fill, and the team has been cycling through unproven American receivers all season. Kenny Lawler's acrimonious departure in 2023 — a contract dispute that dragged through the off-season before he was dealt — left a scar on the organization's relationship with its own players. When Lawler returned to face his former team, the handshakes were cold and the post-game exchanges were clipped. The Bombers' front office has developed a reputation around the league for hardball negotiations, and it's costing them in free agency and in the locker room.

O'Shea's Loyalty Problem: Coaching on Borrowed Time

Mike O'Shea is the longest-tenured head coach in the CFL, and his loyalty to his veterans is both his greatest strength and his fatal flaw. In the Labour Day Classic, O'Shea kept sending out a defence that was visibly gassed in the fourth quarter and overtime. Ridge Texada, dressed for only his second career CFL game, was targeted repeatedly by Trevor Harris and the Saskatchewan offence. The coaching staff's refusal to rotate younger, fresher bodies into the game — or to adjust the defensive scheme when Texada was clearly being exploited — cost Winnipeg the game. Saskatchewan's Tommy Stevens capped a nine-play, 55-yard touchdown drive in the fourth quarter, and the defence floundered again in overtime, giving up 35 yards and a touchdown on five plays.

The departure of offensive coordinator Buck Pierce to the BC Lions in 2025 was the first public crack in the coaching staff's facade. Pierce had been the architect of the offence that made Collaros look like a Hall of Famer. His exit wasn't just a coaching change — it was a signal that the smartest minds in the building saw the writing on the wall. When Pierce left, he took with him the offensive system that masked Collaros's declining mobility and the line's deteriorating protection schemes. The replacement, Jordan Younger, has been unable to replicate the timing and creativity that defined the dynasty-era offence. The result is a unit that ranks near the bottom of the league in explosive plays and red-zone efficiency.

The Saskatchewan Wake-Up Call: A Changing of the Guard

The 32-26 Labour Day Classic loss wasn't just a defeat — it was a changing of the guard in the West Division. Saskatchewan's defence, which had been struggling, came alive against Winnipeg's offence. The Riders' young defensive line got consistent pressure on Collaros without blitzing, and their rebuilt secondary shut down Winnipeg's receivers. Meanwhile, the Bombers' defence — the unit that was supposed to be the backbone of the team — gave up a 72-yard catch-and-run to Daniel Wiebe early, missed tackles at critical moments, and collapsed in overtime when it mattered most. Jaiden Woodbey had a particularly brutal missed tackle on that Wiebe play, a microcosm of a team that no longer executes the fundamentals.

The numbers are damning. Winnipeg has now lost three straight Labour Day Classics to Saskatchewan. The Riders have won the season series two years running. And the Bombers' record through the first half of 2025 puts them on pace for their worst season since 2016 — the year before O'Shea's rebuild began to take shape. The 34,352 fans at Mosaic Stadium watched a Saskatchewan team that is younger, faster, and hungrier. Corey Mace's defensive scheme brought pressure from every angle, while Marc Mueller's offence exploited Winnipeg's aging linebackers in coverage.
