The Bombers' Scheme Is Aging Faster Than Their Roster

October 02, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

Zach Collaros and the Shrinking Middle of the Field

Winnipeg's passing game has always been a timing offence dressed up as a vertical one. Under Buck Pierce, the Bombers built their dropback menu off play-action from the same gap and zone run looks they showed on first down — dagger concepts, deep crossers, and a heavy diet of 15-to-20-yard throws between the numbers. That's the exact band of the field where Collaros is still elite. His anticipation on intermediate throws is the best in the league; he releases before the receiver breaks, which is why he's won two Most Outstanding Player awards and two Grey Cups in Winnipeg.

The problem is what happens when the pocket isn't clean. Collaros is not a scrambler and never has been. His off-platform, outside-the-structure throws are functional, not threatening. So when the Bombers have to keep a running back or an inline tight end in to chip on early downs, the route inventory collapses from five-man concepts to three-man concepts. You can see it on film: the crossers get bracketed, the backside dig is taken away, and Collaros is forced into a tight-window throw to a covered receiver on third-and-long. That's not a quarterback decline. That's a protection-driven compression of the field.

Dalton Schoen's absence compounds it. Schoen was the X who won on option routes and back-shoulder fades — the receiver who made the timing throws easy by being where he was supposed to be. Kenny Lawler wins contested balls but needs time to separate. Nic Demski is a motion and jet-sweep weapon who thrives on manufactured touches. Ontaria Wilson gives them a vertical threat. None of them replace what Schoen did on second-and-7.

Brady Oliveira Is the Entire Offensive Identity

Brady Oliveira has back-to-back 1,000-yard seasons and consecutive Most Outstanding Canadian awards, and he is not a luxury in this offence — he is the load-bearing wall. Winnipeg runs a heavy dose of duo and gap-power with a pulling guard, married to outside zone that sets up the bootleg. Their play-action efficiency is a direct function of down-and-distance. When Oliveira gets them to second-and-3 or shorter, the entire playbook opens: the boot, the naked keeper, the shot play off the run fake.

When he doesn't, the offence becomes predictable in a way that's visible from the press box. Collaros in the gun, 11 personnel, and a defence that can sit on the intermediate crossers because it knows the run isn't a threat. Watch the first two series of any Bombers game this season: if Oliveira is getting three or four yards on first down, Winnipeg is a top-tier offence. If he's getting stuffed on first down, they're a 20-point team. The correlation is almost mechanical.

Oliveira's value in pass protection matters too, and it's the quiet reason the Bombers resist using him as a pure receiver out of the backfield. He's the best blitz-pickup back on the roster. Taking him off the field to spread it out costs them the one player who can buy Collaros an extra half-second.

Richie Hall's Single-High Shell and the Second-and-Medium Problem

Richie Hall has run a single-high, press-man-leaning defence for a decade, and it's been the most consistent structural identity in the CFL. Willie Jefferson aligns wide at the nine-technique and forces the tackle to declare his set. The free safety rotates to the field. The corners are asked to hold up in man on the outside with minimal help.

That structure is only as good as the cornerback room, and Winnipeg's has churned relentlessly — departures, NFL shots, injuries, replacements. When the corners can't hold up, the whole thing leaks. The specific vulnerability is second-and-medium. Offences can run or pass, and Hall's tendency is to stay in his base nickel rather than dial up pressure. That's where opposing coordinators have attacked: run-pass options to the field side, quick game to the boundary, and a steady diet of throws that force the single-high safety to choose.

Where Hall's defence still wins is the red zone. The field compresses, the windows get tight, and the Bombers' aggressive man coverage becomes an asset rather than a liability. Winnipeg's red-zone defence has consistently ranked near the top of the league. The problem is everything between the 20s, where the same aggression gets picked apart by precision passing.

The Trenches Are the Tell

Patrick Neufeld, nominated this week for the Jake Gaudaur Veterans' Award, is the connective tissue of this offence. He's been a Bomber for over a decade, a two-time Grey Cup champion, and the guy who communicates the protection calls. But the offensive line that made Winnipeg's scheme work — Stanley Bryant, Neufeld, Chris Kolankowski, Jermarcus Hardrick, Geoff Gray — is aging out or already gone, and the replacements haven't matched the standard.

Here's the diagnostic: watch how often Winnipeg slides protection to the left tackle, and how often the back stays in. If the Bombers are keeping seven in to protect on first and second down, they've already lost the down. The scheme needs five-man protection and a checkdown to function. When it doesn't get it, Collaros is throwing into coverage and Oliveira is running into eight-man boxes.

Week 18 and the Playoff Math

With the West race tightening and Week 18 carrying playoff implications, the Bombers' path runs through the same two questions they've faced all year: can the offensive line hold up in five-man protection, and can the cornerback room survive in single-high man? Neither is a talent question anymore. Both are structural. Winnipeg's coaching staff has spent a decade building an identity that demands precision from aging parts — and in 2025, the film shows the precision slipping faster than the roster is turning over.
