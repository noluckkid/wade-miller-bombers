Bombers' East Final Autopsy: Protection Failures, Slow Starts, and a Taunting Firestorm

November 08, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Slow Start: Offensive Scheme and Protection Breakdowns

Winnipeg's offence opened the East Final with the urgency of a Sunday morning stroll. The unit punted on four of its first five possessions, two of which were two-and-outs. The only drive that didn't end in a punt came early in the second quarter, when Brady Oliveira coughed up a fumble near midfield. He was initially ruled down, but Montreal challenged and won the takeaway. That sequence encapsulated the first half: Winnipeg's offensive line could not hold up in pass protection, and the play-calling failed to mitigate the pressure.

Zach Collaros, 38, faced an unblocked defender on a four-man rush and was forced to throw an ugly chest pass to Oliveira. He was also sacked by an unblocked Najee Murray, though Montreal sent six rushers and Collaros may have been hot on the play. The protection issues were systemic, not isolated. Winnipeg's offensive line outmuscled the Alouettes in the run game. Oliveira averaged well over seven yards per carry, but that same group was repeatedly beaten in obvious passing situations. Collaros needs a clean pocket to operate at this stage of his career; he is not a scrambler who can evade free rushers.

The play-calling exacerbated the problem. Dorian Singer was targeted only once in the entire first half, while Ontaria Wilson wasn't targeted at all. If Winnipeg was going to win, both receivers needed to be involved. Instead, the Bombers leaned on early-down runs and short routes that allowed Montreal's defence to load the box and blitz with impunity. The Alouettes' defensive coordinator clearly identified Winnipeg's tendency to keep running backs and tight ends in to block, or not block at all, and exploited it with well-timed pressures. Collaros was under siege because the scheme failed to provide hot reads, chip help, or max protection.

Halftime Adjustments and the Vaval Factor

Winnipeg's offence finally woke up before halftime, adding two scores after long returns from Trey Vaval. Sergio Castillo made a 25-yard field goal, and Bryce Perkins punched in a short touchdown plunge to keep the score within reach at 17-13. Those points were manufactured by special teams, not the offence. Vaval's returns gave the Bombers short fields and masked the unit's inefficiency. Without those hidden yards, Winnipeg may have been buried by halftime.

Come the second half, the passing attack finally came to life. Collaros began targeting Singer and Wilson, and the offence moved the ball with more consistency. But the damage was done. Montreal's defence adjusted by playing softer coverage and rallying to the ball, forcing Winnipeg to string together long drives. The Bombers' red-zone issues persisted, they settled for field goals or turned the ball over on downs. The Alouettes, meanwhile, capitalized on short fields created by Winnipeg's early stalls. The final score of 33-23 doesn't reflect how dominant Montreal was for the first 25 minutes.

The X's and O's story is clear: Winnipeg's offensive scheme failed to maximize its personnel. Oliveira is a star, but feeding him 20-plus times while ignoring two dynamic receivers is a recipe for stagnation. Collaros is a rhythm passer who needs quick outlets and clean pockets. The Bombers' coaching staff did not adjust their protection schemes or route combinations until it was too late. Montreal's defence, led by a disciplined front seven, exploited every tendency.

The Taunting Incident: Mental Discipline and Cultural Fallout

Shortly after winning the East Final, Davis Alexander accused Winnipeg defensive lineman David Reese of taunting him about the death of his father, multiple times. "That's f, ing horses," Alexander said. "I don't want to air the guy out, but it wasn't just one time, it wasn't twice. It was multiple times." Alexander lost his father, Matt, to lung cancer in 2024. He showed remarkable composure by walking to the locker room instead of retaliating. Tyson Philpot called the taunts "super disrespectful" and said Reese "doesn't deserve to play in this game if you want to act like that."

Jason Maas acknowledged the accusation, calling it "unsportsmanlike" and noting that "a lot of things get said in football in the heat of emotion." He and Mike O'Shea will discuss it. From a purely analytical standpoint, the incident reflects a lack of mental discipline on Winnipeg's side. Reese's actions may have fired up the Alouettes, who scored 33 points and controlled the game's tempo. Alexander's composure, taking a moment in the locker room, likely helped Montreal stay focused. The Bombers, meanwhile, played with the urgency of a team that expected to win simply by showing up.

Mental errors are as costly as physical ones. Reese's taunts didn't cause the protection breakdowns or the slow start, but they fit a pattern of a team that was not fully locked in. O'Shea has built a culture of discipline in Winnipeg, but this incident suggests cracks. The league may review the matter, and Reese could face discipline. More importantly, the Bombers need to ask themselves why a defensive lineman was more focused on trash talk than on stopping Alexander, who torched them with his legs and arm in the second half.

What It Means: Scheme Fails to Maximize Personnel

Winnipeg's season ends with a clear blueprint for opposing defences: blitz Collaros, dare the receivers to beat man coverage, and load the box against Oliveira. The Bombers' offensive line is better in the run game than in pass protection, and the coaching staff has not schemed around that limitation. Collaros, at 38, cannot absorb free rushers. He needs quick throws, play-action, and moving pockets, none of which were consistently deployed until the second half.

Defensively, Winnipeg's front four failed to generate pressure without blitzing, allowing Alexander to extend plays.
