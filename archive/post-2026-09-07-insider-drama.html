The Maserati Has No Brakes: Inside Winnipeg's Fracturing Locker Room and the Collaros Gamb

September 07, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Keys to a Broken Car

Tommy Condell's "keys to the Maserati" comment was meant to signal trust. Instead, it exposed the problem. The offensive coordinator has handed Collaros unprecedented freedom at the line of scrimmage — no curfew, no backseat driving — and the offence is still seventh in net yardage, seventh in yards per play, and seventh in passing efficiency. That's not a quarterback problem; that's a system problem, and the system is Collaros's to run.

The numbers are damning. Through eight games, Collaros has completed 71.4 percent of his passes for 1,750 yards, eight touchdowns, and four interceptions. Those are middle-of-the-pack numbers for a quarterback making elite money. And the neck injury that cost him four games is the kind of thing that doesn't just go away at 38. Every hit is a reminder that the franchise's most expensive asset is also its most fragile. Behind closed doors, sources say the training staff has flagged Collaros's recovery as a recurring concern — not just the injury itself, but the way he compensates for it by altering his throwing mechanics, which has led to the errant throws and the interceptions that have plagued the offence in key moments.

The deeper issue is what the "Maserati" comment signals to the rest of the roster. Collaros is being given freedom that no other player on the team enjoys. When the offence stalls — as it did in the Labour Day Classic, where Winnipeg managed just 26 points despite the defence giving them multiple short-field opportunities — there's no accountability at the quarterback position. The play-calling is his. The audibles are his. The blame, however, gets spread around. That breeds resentment in a locker room where players are expected to execute their assignments to perfection while the franchise quarterback gets to improvise.

The Secondary Scapegoats

In the Labour Day Classic, the defence held Saskatchewan to 13 points through three quarters. Then the wheels came off. Ridge Texada, dressed for just his second career regular-season game, got fooled on a double-move and then busted a coverage assignment on consecutive plays, allowing Dhel Duncan-Busby to rack up chunk gains that set up two Tommy Stevens touchdown runs. Jaiden Woodbey whiffed on a 72-yard catch-and-run from Daniel Wiebe. Evan Holm took a costly face-masking penalty that extended a drive. The defence didn't lose that game — the offence's inability to sustain drives left them on the field for 40 minutes — but the young defensive backs are the ones getting blamed in the film room.

That's where the locker room starts to fracture. Veterans on defence know the offence is the problem. They see Collaros getting paid top dollar while they're out there gasping for air in the fourth quarter. They see young players like Texada being thrown into the fire because the coaching staff can't find better options — and then being publicly scapegoated when they fail. The CFL's new clock system has made fourth-quarter defensive stops harder league-wide, but Winnipeg's defence is particularly vulnerable because they're exhausted by the time the fourth quarter rolls around. The unit allowed 13 points through 45 minutes in Regina, then surrendered 19 points in the final 15 minutes plus overtime. That's not a talent collapse; that's a fatigue collapse, and the root cause is an offence that can't stay on the field.

The tension is palpable. Multiple defensive veterans have reportedly voiced frustration in team meetings about the offensive game plan, specifically the lack of short, high-percentage passes that would sustain drives and give the defence a breather. Condell's response has been to lean even harder into Collaros's freedom at the line — a decision that has created a quiet but growing rift between the offensive and defensive sides of the ball.

The Contract Clock Is Ticking

Here's the uncomfortable truth: Collaros's contract is a cap anchor. The Bombers have committed significant money to a 38-year-old quarterback with a neck injury and declining production. That money has to come from somewhere — and it's coming from the defensive depth chart. The reason Texada is playing meaningful snaps is that the Bombers couldn't afford to keep better options. The reason the pass rush is thin is that cap space is tied up in the quarterback position. Every dollar spent on Collaros's extension is a dollar not spent on a veteran defensive back who could have prevented the coverage busts that cost Winnipeg the Labour Day Classic.

And the clock is ticking. Collaros's contract runs through 2026, but the Bombers have to decide whether they're building around a 38-year-old or planning for the future. That decision creates tension in the locker room. Young players want to know if they're part of the plan. Veterans want to win now. And the front office is caught in between, trying to thread a needle that's getting narrower every week. The backup quarterback situation is telling: Winnipeg hasn't developed a credible successor, and the lack of a clear succession plan has left the roster in a state of limbo. Players know that if Collaros goes down again — and at 38 with a neck injury, that's a real possibility — the season is over. That uncertainty is corrosive.

The Condell Experiment

Condell was hired this offseason because of his existing relationship with Collaros, whom he coached in Hamilton. That's not a secret — it's the reason he got the job. But that relationship is now a liability. When the offence struggles, Condell can't be objective about his quarterback. He's giving Collaros "keys to the Maserati" while the car is sputtering. The previous offensive coordinator, Buck Pierce, had a different approach — more structure, more discipline. And while Pierce's offence wasn't perfect, it didn't give the quarterback unlimited freedom to check into plays that don't work.
