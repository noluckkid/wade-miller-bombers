The O'Shea Era Ended Because the Scheme Stopped Evolving

November 20, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

The Verdict Was Schematical, Not Sentimental

Wade Miller stood in front of reporters Thursday and said the quiet part out loud: "My role is to look forward for us and not back, and see the direction where we need to head over the next five years or so." That is not the language of a president firing a coach over one loss in the West Final. That is the language of an executive who has watched the same game film for three seasons and concluded the architecture is obsolete.

O'Shea goes out with a 126-86 regular-season record, two Grey Cup rings (2019, 2021), four consecutive championship-game appearances from 2019 through 2023, and two Coach of the Year awards. He also goes out with two years left on the three-year extension Miller handed him after a first-round playoff exit in 2024, a deal that now reads as a hedge that didn't hold. The Bombers' brass evaluated the season and the trajectory, not the résumé.

So let's talk about the trajectory, because it lives in the X's and O's.

Collaros Under Duress: A Passing Game With No Escape Hatch

Winnipeg's offense has been built on the same scaffolding since 2019: huddle, condensed splits, heavy play-action off outside zone, and a vertical shot tree that asks Zach Collaros to hold the ball and take hits. When Stanley Bryant and Jermarcus Hardrick were anchoring the edges and Pat Neufeld was mauling interior defenders, that formula produced explosives at will. In 2025, with a reshuffled front five, it produced something far less flattering, a quarterback who spent far too many snaps operating from a collapsing pocket with no hot answer built into the concept.

Watch the film and the tell is obvious: Winnipeg ran one of the lowest rates of shotgun, quick-game and RPO concepts in the league. Their answer to pressure was never a sight adjustment or a blitz-beater to the slot, it was Collaros escaping. That works when your quarterback is 31 and your tackles are All-Stars. It does not work when the pocket is muddy and defences have learned that a second-and-long against the Bombers means pin your ears back, because the down-and-distance menu is thin. The Bombers were repeatedly behind the sticks on second down in the second half of the season, and their conversion rate in those situations reflected a scheme with no intermediate-middle answer.

Dalton Schoen and Nic Demski remain one of the league's better receiver tandems, and Ontaria Wilson emerged as a genuine vertical threat. But a receiving corps is only as good as the route combinations it's asked to run. Winnipeg's reliance on mirrored verticals and isolation back-shoulder throws meant receivers were winning on talent, not on leverage. Defensive coordinators noticed.

Brady Oliveira Deserved Better Second-Down Geometry

Here's the number that should haunt the offensive staff: Brady Oliveira's rushing efficiency on first down was among the best in the CFL, and his efficiency on second-and-short was fine, but Winnipeg's insistence on running into loaded boxes on first down, then asking Collaros to convert second-and-eight against two-high coverage, is a self-inflicted wound.

Oliveira is a 1,000-yard back who wins with patience and contact balance, not with a burst that erases a bad block. The Bombers' heavy personnel packages, tackle-eligible looks, tight-end-in-line formations, motion that tipped the run strength rather than disguising it, telegraphed run/pass keys to opposing linebackers. Saskatchewan's front seven feasted on it in the West Final, consistently getting a linebacker scraping over the top before the pulling guard had cleared the box. There is no version of that play that works against a defence that knows what's coming.

And when the run was stuffed, the play-action that was supposed to punish aggression never had time to develop, because the protection couldn't hold long enough to sell the fake, let alone throw off it.

Jordan Younger's Defence: Pressure That Outran Its Coverage

The defensive side is more complicated, because Jordan Younger's unit was legitimately good for stretches. Willie Jefferson remains a game-wrecker on the edge, and the Bombers generated pressure at one of the higher rates in the league. The problem ishowthey generated it. Winnipeg blitzed heavily on second down and leaned on man coverage behind it, betting that Jefferson's length and a physical press corner would shorten the quarterback's clock.

When it worked, it was suffocating. When it didn't, the middle of the field opened up like a hangar door. CFL offences eventually figured out that motion and rub concepts could beat the Bombers' man principles, and that the shallow crosser behind a blitzing linebacker was there every single week. Winnipeg's second-half defensive numbers in the intermediate middle, the 10-to-15-yard window between the hashes, were the tell. Add in a red-zone package that lacked a true goalline answer and you get a unit that was excellent between the 20s and leaky where games are decided.

That's a coaching problem, not a personnel problem. And it's precisely the kind of problem a new head coach with a different pressure philosophy, someone like Wally Buono protégé Mike Benevides, whose B.C. defences marry pressure to coverage, can fix without a rebuild.

The West Final Was the Audition Tape

Winnipeg came within a game of the Grey Cup, and it's worth being precise about why they lost. Against Saskatchewan, the Riders rushed four, dropped eight, and dared the Bombers to throw into the intermediate window. Winnipeg's response was to keep running into light boxes and keep asking Collaros to buy time with his legs. That is not a talent deficit. That is a scheme that ran out of answers in the biggest moment of the season.

Miller has now opened the search externally, and the candidate profile matters enormously.
