The Roberts Fit: Winnipeg Bets on Range to Fix Its Second-Level Leaks

November 25, 2026 · AI-synthesized analysis

Why a 233-Pound Run-and-Chase Backer Fits Younger's System

The temptation is to file the Jacob Roberts signing under "depth." That misreads it. A one-year deal for a 25-year-old American linebacker, 6-1, 233, born September 2001, is a scheme bet, and it lands in the part of Winnipeg's defense that has quietly aged out of its own identity. The Bombers built a decade of dominance on second-level players who could diagnose a run fit in two steps and still carry a slot receiver on the wide side. Since Adam Bighill's retirement and Kyrie Wilson's repeated soft-tissue setbacks, that profile has been replaced by committee work: bigger bodies on early downs, faster bodies on second-and-long, and a lot of substitution tell.

Roberts' Calgary tape is that of a WILL/MIKE hybrid who plays with a low pad level and a genuinely quick trigger. The raw line, 93 defensive tackles in 18 games, 4 sacks, 1 forced fumble, plus 9 special-teams tackles, works out to roughly 5.2 defensive stops per game, which in a 12-man, three-down league is the number you want from a player asked to make the pile move inside and chase the waggle to the boundary. The four sacks matter as much as the tackle volume: they suggest a coordinator comfortable blitzing him through the A-gap, which is exactly where Jordan Younger's pressure packages live when he walks the MIKE up and brings a second-level defender behind a twisting end.

Where Roberts should help immediately is unscouted looks. CFL motion, legal, unlimited, and constant, forces linebackers to declare early. Winnipeg's second level declared late too often in 2026, and opposing coordinators punished it with the same two concepts on loop: pin-pull to the field side and the play-action boot off it. A linebacker who can scrape over the top of a down block and still get to the quarterback's backside is the antidote. Roberts was that player in Calgary.

Film: What 93 Tackles in Calgary Actually Tell You

Tackle totals lie in this league, so read the context. Roberts wasn't a protected MIKE behind two 300-pounders; he played in a Stampeders front that asked him to take on lead blocks and run to the ball as the clean-up man. That's a different job description than what he'll get in Winnipeg, where the defensive line is built to keep the second level clean. The translation should be favorable, even if the volume drops.

The coverage piece is the real question, and his Minnesota tape is more instructive than his college résumé. In three preseason games with the Vikings he posted 12 total tackles, a pass knockdown and an interception before hyperextending his knee in Denver on August 28 and losing his job to an injury settlement two days later. Twelve tackles in three exhibition games is an absurd rate, but it's the interception and knockdown that matter here: both came from spot-drop and quarters responsibilities, meaning he read a quarterback's eyes and drove on the ball rather than simply running to a spot. CFL defenses ask linebackers to do more of that than NFL ones do, not less, the field is 65 yards wide and the second level has to cover all of it.

The honest concern is straight-line speed in space. At 233 pounds, Roberts wins with anticipation, not recovery. If Younger asks him to play man against a slotback on the short side, that's a mismatch. If he's asked to wall off the curl-flat window in Cover 3 and match the running back on checkdowns, he's a plus player.

The Ratio Domino and the 2026 Blueprint

Here's the structural tension. Winnipeg has long preferred Canadian linebackers to solve ratio. Shayne Gauthier's value was never the stat line, it was the roster math. Signing an American starter at the position pushes a Canadian somewhere else, likely onto the offensive line or into a receiving package. That's manageable, but it isn't free, and it's why the one-year term is smart: it's a prove-it contract that doesn't handcuff the ratio plan if he doesn't win the job in camp.

Pair this with the Jamieson Sheahan re-signing and you can see the front office's off-season philosophy forming, invest in the hidden yards. Sheahan pins teams and flips fields; Roberts erases the explosive plays that come from a defense being a half-step slow at the second level. Add five All-CFL selections returning and the roster core is intact. The gaps being filled are the ones that don't show up in a highlight package but do show up on third down.

The Health Caveat

The knee is the whole bet. A hyperextension in late August that ends in an injury settlement means NFL teams flagged something, and the UFL's Orlando Storm spending a third-round pick on him last week means someone else was willing to bet on the medicals too. If Roberts is the player who put up 93 tackles and nine special-teams stops in 18 games, Winnipeg got a starter on a one-year deal in late November, the cheapest kind of roster upgrade. If the knee limits his change of direction, he's a two-down thumper in a league that increasingly can't afford them.

Watch the first week of training camp, not the first game. Specifically: whether he's aligned at MIKE or WILL in the base package, and whether he stays on the field in the dime. That answer tells you whether this signing was about repairing the run defense or just about depth.
