The Tezino Trade Exposes Bombers' Roster Blind Spot — And Ottawa Just Got a Steal

September 03, 2026 · AI-synthesized from real news sources

The Winnipeg Blue Bombers just handed the Ottawa Redblacks a starting-caliber American linebacker for the CFL rights to a quarterback who will never take a snap in Winnipeg. On paper, it's a depth move. In reality, it's a confession.

When the Bombers shipped Kyahva Tezino to Ottawa for the rights to QB Ty Pennington — a player buried on Ottawa's depth chart — they didn't just make a trade. They told us exactly how they view their own roster: that defensive playmakers are expendable, that the quarterback position is the only one that matters, and that a 2026 season with Grey Cup aspirations can afford to give away proven talent for futures that may never materialize.

That's a dangerous way to run a football team. And the numbers back it up.

The Anatomy of a Lopsided Deal

Let's be clear about what Winnipeg gave up. Tezino isn't a practice roster afterthought. He's a linebacker who has shown he can play at this level — the kind of player who makes tackles in traffic, covers running backs out of the backfield, and provides the physical presence that championship defenses are built around. The Bombers' defense has been the backbone of their recent success, and Tezino was part of that fabric.

In return, they get the rights to Ty Pennington. A quarterback. On Ottawa's roster. Behind their starter. The Redblacks essentially traded a player who could help them win now for a lottery ticket at the game's most important position — and they did it because Winnipeg was willing to make that deal.

This isn't a criticism of Pennington as a prospect. It's a criticism of the value proposition. The Bombers have Zach Collaros. They have a quarterback situation that, while not perfect, isn't desperate enough to justify giving away defensive starters. If Winnipeg wanted quarterback depth, there are cheaper ways to acquire it than trading away players who can actually help you win in November.

The Numbers Don't Lie

Look at the broader context. The Bombers just dismantled the Montreal Alouettes 44-28 in Week 13 — a statement win that announced Winnipeg as a legitimate contender. That game was won on the strength of offensive explosion, yes, but also on defensive stops at critical moments. The defense held Montreal's imposing offence in check long enough for the offence to build a lead. That's what good defensive units do.

Now imagine that same defense without Tezino in a playoff game. Imagine a third-down conversion in the West Final where the Bombers need one stop, and the linebacker who would have made that play is wearing an Ottawa uniform. That's the risk Winnipeg just accepted.

And here's the thing: the Bombers aren't alone in this thinking. The CFL has a systemic problem with undervaluing defensive players. The league's salary structure rewards quarterbacks and offensive skill positions, while defensive playmakers — the guys who actually win championships — are treated as interchangeable parts. The Tezino trade is just the latest example of this myopia.

Consider the numbers. The Bombers' defence has been a top-tier unit for years, and their defensive players have consistently outperformed their contracts. Tezino was one of those players — a guy who produced more than his salary suggested, which should make him more valuable, not less. Instead, Winnipeg treated him as surplus.

The Counter-Argument: Cap Space and Asset Management

I can already hear the counter-arguments. The Bombers' front office would say they're managing the salary cap. They'd say Tezino's contract was becoming burdensome, that they needed to free up space for other moves, that acquiring Pennington's rights gives them future flexibility at the most important position in sports.

They'd also point to the Bombers' track record. This is a franchise that has been to multiple Grey Cups. They've earned the benefit of the doubt when it comes to roster decisions. General Manager Kyle Walters has built a winner, and he deserves credit for that.

But here's the problem with that argument: it assumes the Bombers' success is sustainable regardless of their defensive depth. It assumes that because they've won before, they'll win again. And it ignores the reality that championship windows close quickly in the CFL. The Bombers have a window right now. They have a quarterback who can win, a defence that can dominate, and a fan base that expects nothing less than a Grey Cup. Trading away defensive starters for future assets is how you let that window close.

And let's be honest about the Pennington acquisition. The Redblacks were willing to part with his rights for a reason. If he were a franchise-caliber quarterback, Ottawa wouldn't have traded him for a linebacker. The Bombers didn't acquire a solution; they acquired a maybe. And they paid for it with a player who is a definite.

What This Means for the Stretch Run

The Bombers are 44-28 winners over Montreal, and they're positioned for a playoff run. But the Tezino trade is a reminder that no lead is safe, no roster is perfect, and no front office is infallible.

Here's my prediction: this trade will look worse in December than it does in August. If the Bombers make the Grey Cup and win, nobody will remember Tezino's name. But if they lose a close game in the playoffs — a game where they needed one more defensive stop — this trade will be the story. It will be the move that cost them a championship.

And here's my call to action for CFL fans: stop accepting the narrative that defensive players are expendable. Start asking questions when your team trades away proven talent for futures. The Bombers just made a mistake. The only question is whether they'll pay for it when it matters most.

Ottawa, meanwhile, just got a steal. The Redblacks acquired a player who can help them immediately, and they gave up nothing they'll miss. That's how you build a winner — by recognizing value when you see it, even when the other team doesn't.
