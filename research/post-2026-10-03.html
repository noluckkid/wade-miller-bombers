How Winnipeg's 29-Year Grey Cup Wait Ended — and Why It Still Matters

October 03, 2026 · AI-synthesized from real news sources

This week, the Winnipeg Blue Bombers board a plane for Montreal needing a win to keep their season alive. They do it a week after a 30-28 loss to Toronto, with two starters returning to the lineup and receiver Kian Schaffer-Baker freshly named among nine nominees for the Jake Gaudaur Veterans' Award. The 113th Grey Cup playoff scenarios are already circulating, and the Alouettes are chasing first place in the East.

A generation ago, a week like this would have felt like a death sentence in Winnipeg. It doesn't anymore. The reason is a single afternoon in Calgary, seven years ago — and the 29 years of failure that made it possible.

The Week That Would Have Broken Them Once

November 24, 2019. McMahon Stadium, Calgary. The Blue Bombers beat the Hamilton Tiger-Cats 33-12 in the 107th Grey Cup, ending a championship drought that had spanned four decades and outlasted an entire generation of season-ticket holders.

The numbers were absurd for a team that limped into the postseason at 11-7. Winnipeg finished third in the West, crossed over to the East Division playoffs, and then won three straight road games: 35-14 in Montreal, 31-14 in Hamilton, and 33-12 in the Grey Cup itself. No crossover team had ever won the Grey Cup. None has since.

Andrew Harris, the Winnipeg-born running back, ran for 134 yards and caught five passes for 35 more, scoring a touchdown. He became the first player in Grey Cup history to be named both Most Valuable Player and Most Valuable Canadian in the same game. Zach Collaros, acquired from Toronto at the October 9 trade deadline for a conditional draft pick, started all three playoff games. Mike O'Shea, hired in December 2013, won his first championship in his sixth season.

Twenty-nine years of waiting, ended by a team nobody picked, on the road, three times over.

Twenty-Nine Years in the Wilderness

To understand what 2019 meant, you have to sit with 1990.

On November 25, 1990, at BC Place, the Bombers demolished the Edmonton Eskimos 50-11 in the 78th Grey Cup — still the most lopsided championship game in CFL history. Tom Burgess directed an offence that hung 50 points on Edmonton. Greg Battle, the CFL's Most Outstanding Defensive Player in both 1990 and 1991, anchored a defence that never let the Eskimos breathe. Mike Riley was the head coach. Chris Walby was at left tackle. Bob Cameron was punting.

Everyone assumed there would be more. There wasn't.

Winnipeg lost the 1992 Grey Cup to Calgary 24-10, and the 1993 Grey Cup to Edmonton 33-23. Then came the collapse: 4-14 in 1997 and 3-15 in 1998 under Jeff Reinebold, still the darkest stretch in modern franchise history. The Bombers played in the East Division from 2005 to 2013 while Ottawa sat dormant. They lost the 2001 Grey Cup to Calgary 27-19 despite a 14-4 record and Khari Jones's Most Outstanding Player season. They lost the 2007 Grey Cup to Saskatchewan 23-19 after Kevin Glenn broke his arm in the East Final and Ryan Dinwiddie was forced into the biggest start of his life. They lost the 2011 Grey Cup to BC 34-23.

Five Grey Cup appearances between 1990 and 2019. Five losses. In 2013, the first season at what was then Investors Group Field, the Bombers went 3-15.

The 2019 title did not come from a sudden infusion of talent. It came from institutional patience. Winnipeg kept O'Shea through a 7-11 debut in 2014 and a 5-13 season in 2015. It kept Kyle Walters, elevated to general manager in 2013. It built a Canadian core — Harris, receiver Nic Demski, linebacker Adam Bighill — and supplemented it with free-agent veterans like Willie Jefferson, signed in February 2019.

What the 2019 Blueprint Still Teaches

The 2019 championship wasn't the end of the story. It was the beginning of the most sustained run of relevance in modern Bomber history: five consecutive Grey Cup appearances from 2019 through 2024, with a second title in 2021 — a 33-25 overtime win over Hamilton at Tim Hortons Field.

That run has since given way to the harder work of reloading. As the 2026 season reaches Week 18, the Bombers are in a familiar position: fighting for their playoff lives, coming off that 30-28 loss to the Argonauts, and preparing for a trip to Montreal with reinforcements returning. Meanwhile, Schaffer-Baker's Gaudaur nomination carries a name that belongs to this franchise. Jake Gaudaur played for the Bombers in the late 1940s before serving as CFL commissioner from 1968 to 1984; the award honours Canadian players who demonstrate his leadership and perseverance.

That's the thread. The Bombers have never been built on flash. They've been built on continuity, on Canadian talent, and on the stubborn belief that November is the only month that matters.

What Fans Should Take From It

Three lessons from the 29-year wait.

First: championships are not linear. The 1990 team was a juggernaut that won by 39 points. The 2019 team was a third-place wild card that won three road games by a combined 59. The best regular-season team does not always win the Grey Cup. The healthiest and hottest team in November usually does.

Second: patience with a coach is a strategy, not a weakness. O'Shea went 7-11, then 5-13, before he went to five straight Grey Cups. Most franchises would have fired him after 2015. Winnipeg didn't, and it changed the decade. That same patience is being tested again right now, in a Week 18 where the margin between a playoff run and an early offseason is a single road game in Montreal.

Third: the drought is over, but it is never far away. The Bombers of 2026 are not the Bombers of 2019 — different quarterback room, different secondary, different questions. What remains is the standard: make the playoffs, get hot, and let the wind at Princess Auto Stadium do the rest.

Twenty-nine years is a long time. The fans who waited through it know something the rest of the league sometimes forgets: in Winnipeg, November is not the end of the season. It's the whole point.
