Winnipeg and Saskatchewan Are Paying for 2022, Not 2025

October 06, 2026 · AI-synthesized from real news sources

Two of the CFL's flagship franchises are in free fall, and the comfortable explanation — injuries, bad bounces, a quarterback who suddenly forgot how to play — is the wrong one. Winnipeg and Saskatchewan are sliding because their front offices spent three years paying market-plus for a core that has since aged out of its prime. The bill came due in 2025, and it arrived in the form of a 37-10 humiliation in Montreal.

This is not a talent problem. It's a cap-allocation problem. And the transaction wire tells the story more honestly than the standings do.

Winnipeg's Cap Sheet Is a Museum of 2022

Start with the scoreboard, because it's blunt. The Blue Bombers went into Montreal and got dismantled, 37-10, in a game that 3DownNation's own coverage described in terms that should terrify a fan base: helpless. Not unlucky. Not out-executed on a couple of plays. Helpless.

That word is a roster-construction verdict, not a game verdict. Winnipeg's model for half a decade has been continuity — keep the band together, pay the guys who won you two Grey Cups, trust the locker room. It's an admirable philosophy and it worked beautifully when those players were 26 and the cap was smaller. The problem is that the contracts signed in 2021 and 2022 were priced against a cap that has since climbed past $5.5 million, and the players who signed them have since moved to the wrong side of 30. You don't get a discount for loyalty. You get a cap hit.

The result is a roster shaped like an hourglass: a cluster of expensive, respected veterans at the top, a pile of minimum-salary bodies at the bottom, and almost nothing in the middle. In a league where the cap sits north of $5.5 million and a starting quarterback can eat eight to ten percent of it, the middle class is where you win. Winnipeg doesn't have one.

When a starter goes down in Winnipeg, the drop-off isn't a step. It's a cliff. That's what a top-heavy cap sheet buys you.

Watch the September transaction wire for the tell. Montreal, sitting in a genuine race for first in the East, added defensive lineman Dylan Wynn — a rotational interior piece, exactly the kind of depth signing a healthy cap allows. B.C. locked up Canadian defensive back Cristophe Beaulieu on a one-year extension — a ratio play, a special-teams play, a depth play. Those are the moves of teams with room to maneuver. Winnipeg's moves have been reactive for two seasons.

Saskatchewan's Lauther Trade Is a Panic Signal

Now Saskatchewan. The Roughriders reacquired kicker Brett Lauther in a trade with the Redblacks — a player they let walk in the first place — while the 3DownNation power rankings flagged their second-half slide. Read those two facts together and you have a front office that misjudged its own window.

Spending trade assets on a kicker in the stretch run is not aggressive. It's an admission. Kicking is the cheapest, most replaceable position on a CFL roster; if you're paying a premium in draft capital or negotiation-list assets to fix it in October, your February plan failed. The Riders are publicly targeting the postseason, and they should be — but they're patching a hull, not rebuilding a boat.

There's a pattern here that connects both franchises: they're managing for the team they were, not the team they are. Saskatchewan and Winnipeg have both been good enough, recently enough, that their decision-makers can justify one more run. One more run is how you end up with a 34-year-old starter on a legacy contract and a rookie backing him up.

The Counter-Argument: Injuries and Quarterback Play

The standard defense is that this is all noise — that Winnipeg has been gutted by injuries and Saskatchewan's slide is just regression, and that if you squint, both teams are a healthy quarterback away. I've heard it. It's wrong, and here's why.

Every team in this league deals with injuries. B.C. has weathered them and still put Nathan Rourke atop the September player-of-the-month list. Montreal has cycled through its own quarterback questions and is still eyeing first place in the East. Toronto is the actual quarterback crisis of 2025 — Chad Kelly out with a hand injury, Nick Arbuckle starting against the Lions — and the Argonauts remain relevant because their cap sheet lets them absorb a $500,000-ish starter contributing nothing on game day. That's the difference between a bad break and a bad structure.

The other version of the counter-argument is sentimental: you can't win without stars. Tell that to the 2023 Alouettes. Montreal won a Grey Cup with a roster built on value contracts and a quarterback on a team-friendly deal, then reinvested the savings into depth. Meanwhile Hamilton is preparing for another season of Bo Levi Mitchell — a future Hall of Famer who is openly intent on returning — which is the same trap in a different jersey. Paying for the name is not the same as paying for the production.

The Bill Comes Due in November

Here's my call. When the 113th Grey Cup playoffs kick off on Oct. 31, at least one of Winnipeg and Saskatchewan loses in the divisional round — and it won't be because of a bad bounce. It'll be because a depth piece got beaten on a third-and-short in the fourth quarter, which is exactly where thin rosters show up.

The team that wins the West will be the one with the best ratio of cap dollars to snaps played, not the biggest names on the marquee. That's been true in this league for a decade, and it's about to be proven again.

The harder truth is for the men in the front offices. Loyalty is a value, not a strategy. Winnipeg and Saskatchewan have spent three years confusing the two, and the standings are finally keeping score. If either franchise runs it back in 2026 with the same core on the same money, the slide won't be a slide. It'll be a rebuild with a head start nobody noticed.
