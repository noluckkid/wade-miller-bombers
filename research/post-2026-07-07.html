The Sellout Streak That Defines a City

On a chilly November evening in 2024, as the final seconds ticked off the clock at IG Field, a father hoisted his young daughter onto his shoulders. She wore a tiny blue-and-gold jersey, two sizes too big, and waved a foam finger that was almost as tall as she was. The Bombers had just secured another home playoff game, and the crowd of 33,000—every seat filled—erupted in a roar that could be heard across the Red River.

That moment, captured in a photo that went viral across Manitoba social media, is just one snapshot of a phenomenon that has become the envy of the Canadian Football League. As of the 2025 season, the Winnipeg Blue Bombers had sold out 68 consecutive regular-season home games—a streak stretching back to 2016, before the COVID-19 pandemic, before the team’s four straight Grey Cup appearances. It’s a run that has shattered franchise records and stands as the longest active sellout streak in the CFL.

“It’s not just about football,” says longtime season-ticket holder Margaret Kowalski, who has attended games with her husband since 1982. “It’s about seeing your neighbours, your coworkers, your kids’ teachers. It’s the one place where everyone—from the CEO to the electrician—is cheering for the same thing.”

That sense of unity is palpable on game days. The parking lots surrounding IG Field transform into a sea of tailgating parties, where the smell of smoked sausages and the sound of laughter mix with the distant thud of a practice punt. Families set up lawn chairs, kids toss footballs, and strangers become friends over shared plates of perogies. It’s a tradition that has become as essential to Winnipeg’s identity as the Polar Bear Swim or the Folk Festival.

Youth Football and Community Roots

The Bombers’ impact, however, extends far beyond the 60 minutes on the field. The team’s community investment program, Bombers for Life, has poured over $1.2 million into local initiatives since 2019, with a heavy focus on youth football. The Bombers Youth Football League, launched in partnership with Football Manitoba, now reaches more than 4,000 kids annually across the province—from inner-city Winnipeg to remote northern communities like Thompson and Churchill.

“For a kid in a small town, seeing a Blue Bomber walk into their school is like seeing a superhero,” says Mark Delaney, a physical education teacher at École Viscount Alexander in Winnipeg. “It shows them that football isn’t just something you watch on TV—it’s something they can be part of.”

The program provides equipment, coaching clinics, and scholarships. In 2023 alone, the Bombers donated over 500 sets of shoulder pads and helmets to under-resourced schools. They also run a Flag Football in Schools program that has introduced the sport to more than 10,000 students since 2020.

But the impact goes deeper than gear. Players regularly visit classrooms to read to children, talk about the importance of teamwork, and share their own stories of perseverance. Defensive back Deatrick Nichols, a fan favourite known for his infectious smile, once spent an entire afternoon at a Winnipeg hospital, visiting sick children in the pediatric ward. “These kids are fighting battles harder than any I’ve faced on the field,” he said afterward. “If I can make one of them smile, it’s a win.”

Players Who Give Back

Nichols is far from alone. The Bombers roster is filled with players who treat community service as a core part of their job. Quarterback Zach Collaros and his wife, Nicole, have been active supporters of the Children’s Hospital Foundation of Manitoba, hosting annual visits and fundraising events. Offensive lineman Stanley Bryant, a future Hall of Famer, runs a free football camp every summer for underprivileged youth in his hometown of Lake City, South Carolina—but he also makes time for similar camps in Winnipeg.

“These guys understand that they’re role models,” says Blue Bombers President and CEO Wade Miller. “They don’t just wear the logo—they live it. They show up at community events, they mentor kids, they donate their time and money. It’s a culture that starts at the top and flows through every player who puts on that jersey.”

That culture was on full display in 2025 when the team launched the Bombers Book Club, partnering with Winnipeg Public Library to distribute free books to children at every home game. More than 15,000 books were given away in the first season alone. “It’s about building a community that reads together,” says Miller. “Football brings us together, but literacy builds a future.”

A Province's Pride

To understand what the Blue Bombers mean to Manitoba, you have to look beyond the win-loss column. The team is a source of pride for a province that often feels overlooked by the rest of Canada. When the Bombers ended a 29-year Grey Cup drought in 2019, the victory parade drew an estimated 60,000 people to downtown Winnipeg—on a workday, in late November, with temperatures hovering around -15°C.

“That day, we weren’t just celebrating a football team,” recalls Winnipeg Mayor Scott Gillingham. “We were celebrating resilience. We were celebrating a city that refuses to quit. The Bombers are a reflection of who we are: tough, loyal, and always ready to rally together.”

Economically, the team’s success has been a boon for the city. A 2023 study by the University of Winnipeg estimated that the Bombers generate over $80 million in annual economic activity, supporting hundreds of local jobs. The sellout streak alone means consistent revenue for nearby businesses—restaurants, hotels, and shops that depend on game-day traffic.

But the real value can’t be measured in dollars. It’s in the way a grandmother in Steinbach watches every game with her grandson on FaceTime. It’s in the way a newcomer to Canada, maybe from Nigeria or the Philippines, finds a community in the stands. It’s in the way a province of 1.4 million people packs a 33,000-seat stadium, week after week, year after year, because the Blue Bombers are more than a team—they’re family.

As the sun sets over IG Field and the final whistle blows, the fans don’t rush for the exits. They linger, sharing high-fives, snapping photos, and soaking in the moment. Because for a few hours on a summer or autumn evening, Winnipeg isn’t just a city on the Prairies. It’s the centre of the football universe. And that’s something worth celebrating.
