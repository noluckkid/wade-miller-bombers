The Bombers' Collaros Charade Is a Front-Office Confession

November 07, 2026 · AI-synthesized from real news sources

The Winnipeg Blue Bombers are 60 minutes from a Grey Cup, and every single thing about how they got here argues they shouldn't be. They crossed over from the West. They needed a one-score escape — 25-22 — to survive Toronto in the East Semi. And now, heading into the Eastern Final in Montreal, they've listed Zach Collaros as their starting quarterback with a game-time-decision tag after he missed the final practice of the week.

That last part isn't gamesmanship. It's a confession. For four years, the Bombers have been the CFL's model of continuity — same quarterback, same head coach, same core, same answers at the podium. The Collaros designation is what continuity looks like when it curdles into inertia. Winnipeg isn't managing a quarterback decision. It's avoiding one.

A Crossover Team Wearing a Dynasty's Uniform

Let's start with the record, because the narrative and the standings are telling two different stories.

This is a team that finished fourth in the West and only kept playing because the crossover rule let it in the back door. That's the actual résumé. The Bombers' last two games — a 34-10 thumping of B.C. and that 25-22 squeaker over the Argos — have been sold as a team heating up at the right time. The B.C. game was a regular-season finale against a Lions team that had already been eliminated from relevance. The Toronto game was decided by three points and one or two plays.

Strip away the logo and what's left is a team whose identity has quietly flipped. Jarell Broxton just won Most Outstanding Offensive Lineman — a deserved award, and also a diagnosis. Winnipeg's offense is now built to run the ball behind an elite front and ask its quarterback to manage, not to win. Brady Oliveira is the engine. Collaros is the steering. When the steering is questionable, you don't ask the engine to drive.

Now consider the money. The CFL's cap sits in the high fives, and a starting quarterback's contract eats somewhere north of 10 per cent of it — in Winnipeg's case, likely more. When you commit that percentage to a 30-something quarterback, you are buying certainty. You are paying a premium to not have a game-time decision in the division final. The Bombers paid for certainty and got ambiguity, and Broxton's award just repriced the one unit that actually carries this team.

The "Game-Time Decision" Is the Tell

To be fair to the Bombers' football operations staff: the GTD label is partly administrative. You have to declare a starter. You can't leave the line blank. Listing Collaros and letting the week play out is standard practice across the league, and there's real value in forcing Montreal to prepare for two quarterbacks.

But there's a difference between gamesmanship and self-deception, and the tell here is the sequencing. Collaros didn't take the final practice. Chris Streveler — the guy who would actually start — needed every rep he could get. When a team's public posture is "our starter is our starter" while its private reality is "our starter hasn't thrown a live rep since [insert day]," that's not a competitive advantage. That's a locker room reading the room and drawing its own conclusions. Ask any player: they know who's healthy before the injury report does.

And notice the collateral. Collaros and Oliveira are both listed as questionable. That's your quarterback and your best offensive player, on the road, against a defense that just watched its own quarterback — Alexander — accept the Most Outstanding Player award. If you're Winnipeg, the honest framing of this week is: we may be down our two most important offensive pieces and we're still going to Montreal to win. Instead the framing is a coy non-answer that treats clarity as a competitive liability. Clarity is free. Winnipeg is paying for fog.

The Counterargument Doesn't Survive Contact — and Maas Knows It

Here's the other side, and it's not stupid: you don't bench a two-time Grey Cup champion in a division final. Collaros has won the biggest games this league has. He's the reason that dynasty exists. Veteran quarterbacks get the benefit of the doubt because the benefit of the doubt is usually correct in November.

It's a good argument, and it's built on a roster that no longer exists. The 2019 and 2021 titles were won by a team that could win a shootout. This team can't. It wins by controlling the line, running the ball, and keeping the score in the low 20s. That profile demands a quarterback who can execute a specific, limited game plan — not a quarterback discovering the game plan in real time on a bad wheel in front of 20,000 people at Percival Molson. There's no version of this where an under-practiced Collaros is better than a fully-repped Streveler plus an honest game plan. There's only a version where the coaches feel safer, and safety is not the same as winning.

Meanwhile, Jason Maas gave the most revealing answer of the week. Asked about Oliveira's pressure comments, the Alouettes' head coach shrugged it off entirely.

"I could care less."

Read that again. Maas didn't take the bait. He didn't manufacture a rivalry or pin a quote to the bulletin board. He treated Oliveira's needle like background noise, because he coaches the team with the MOP under centre and home field and no injury report drama. That's what confidence sounds like. Winnipeg spent the week generating pressure narratives about Montreal. Montreal spent the week ignoring them.

And the awards circuit quietly underlined the gap: the league's Most Outstanding Defensive Player, Darnell Sankey, is at home this week. The individual honours are spread around. The teams still playing are the ones whose quarterback situations are, at minimum, known.

The prediction: Collaros either doesn't start or starts and is pulled by the third quarter, Montreal wins by double digits, and Alexander makes the MOP vote look like the easiest call of the year. Then Winnipeg spends an offseason finally answering the question it spent this week dodging — because you can list a starter with a GTD designation, but you can't list a plan B that doesn't exist. René Paredes just walked away after a career of never making anyone wait for an answer. The Bombers owe their fans the same courtesy.
