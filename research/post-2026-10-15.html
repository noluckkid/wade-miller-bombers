Ottawa's Winless Season Is the CFL's Quarterback Economy Failing Out Loud

October 15, 2026 · AI-synthesized from real news sources

Two quarterbacks in two weeks. That's the Ottawa Redblacks' 2026 season in miniature: McLeod Bethel-Thompson gets the start against the B.C. Lions, the team stays winless, and by Friday the job belongs to Bryson Barnes against Toronto. No controversy. No plan. Just triage.

Here's the thesis, and it isn't really about Ottawa: the CFL has built a quarterback economy that rewards hoarding and punishes development. Veterans get recycled, young arms get buried on practice rosters, and when the bill comes due — as it has in the nation's capital — everyone acts surprised. The Redblacks are the loudest symptom. They are not the disease.

Ottawa Didn't Lose a Season — They Lost a Decade

Look at what the Bethel-Thompson-to-Barnes pivot actually communicates. Bethel-Thompson is a late-30s career journeyman — a competent, well-travelled bridge quarterback who has now been asked to hold down multiple franchises. Ottawa signed him because he was available, affordable, and wouldn't embarrass anyone in the film room. That's not a quarterback decision. That's a risk-management decision dressed up as one.

Then the bridge collapsed, and the emergency exit is Barnes — a young passer getting his shot not because the organization believes in him, but because there is literally nothing left to protect. This is how the CFL's developmental pipeline dies: not with a dramatic cut, but with a depth chart that only opens when the season is already gone.

And notice the roster churn around the position. This week alone, the Lions added veteran American offensive lineman Lorenzo Thompson to the practice roster, and the Alouettes signed American running back Marcus Major Jr. Every week, CFL teams cycle through American bodies looking for a spark. It's a treadmill. The players change; the philosophy doesn't.

The CFL doesn't have a quarterback shortage. It has a quarterback allocation problem — and Ottawa is what happens when you allocate badly for a decade and then panic.

Collaros, Kelly, and the Myth of the Win-Now Quarterback

Now look at the other end of the spectrum. Winnipeg names Zach Collaros the starter for a crucial Week 20 matchup against Calgary. Collaros, north of 35 and coming off a season that has been anything but smooth, "turns back the clock" and culls the Stampeders 33-28. The Bombers rise in the power rankings. Everyone exhales.

It's a great story. It's also a confession. Winnipeg — a model franchise, a team that can clinch a playoff spot this week — has no succession plan behind a quarterback in his late thirties. When Collaros is healthy, they're contenders. When he isn't, they're a coin flip. That's not a roster. That's a bet.

Toronto is running the same play with a different deck. Chad Kelly is taking first-team reps again, and the Argonauts are betting that elite arm talent outweighs everything else. Maybe they're right. But notice what both situations have in common: the league's two marquee quarterback stories in Week 20 are a 38-year-old turning back the clock and a reclamation project getting another chance. That is not a talent pipeline. That is a recycling program.

Meanwhile, the CFL just confirmed 139 first-year players in its 2026 rookie class. That's a massive influx of bodies — and the number of them who will take a meaningful snap at quarterback over the next three seasons is functionally zero. The league is importing volume while starving the one position that actually decides games.

The Counter-Argument: Veterans Win Grey Cups

Fine. Let's take the other side seriously, because it's the argument every CFL front office makes in the war room: veteran quarterbacks win championships. The last decade of Grey Cup winners is littered with experienced passers who had been around the block. Why gamble on a kid when the margin between first and fourth in the West is a single game?

Because that argument is survivorship bias wearing a ring. Veterans win Grey Cups because they're the only ones given the runway to do it. The sample is contaminated. Every franchise free-rides on the one team that actually develops a passer, then points at the result as proof the model works. It doesn't. It proves the model is a lottery with one winner and eight losers.

And the cost is real. In a league where the salary cap barely clears $5 million, paying premium money to a declining asset isn't just sentimental — it's structural. It eats the room you'd need to sign depth, to protect the passer, to build a defense that can survive a three-and-out. When Collaros goes down, Winnipeg doesn't fall back on a plan. It falls back on hope. We've seen it.

The ratio rules and the short season make coaches risk-averse. Understood. But risk aversion is a choice, and the CFL has made it collectively for twenty years.

The Bottom Line

Here's my prediction: Ottawa finishes winless, or close enough that the distinction stops mattering. And the post-mortem will blame the quarterback room, the offensive line, the coordinator, the weather. It will blame everything except the philosophy that produced the mess.

The fix isn't a saviour under centre. It's a two-year runway for somebody young — real reps, real patience, real tolerance for interceptions in a lost season. The teams that figure this out first will own the next decade. The teams that don't will keep calling Bethel-Thompson.

Meanwhile, the league office is busy. Greg Dick was just appointed CFL deputy commissioner, and the 113th Grey Cup playoff picture is sharpening in the West. Good. Structure matters. But no broadcast deal or expansion blueprint fixes a league that can't develop the one position its fans pay to watch. Ottawa isn't a warning. It's a mirror.
