Twenty-Nine Years: How Winnipeg Ended Its Longest Winter

November 05, 2026 · AI-synthesized from real news sources

Zach Collaros is on the practice report, limited, ribs unreliable. Jarell Broxton has just been named the CFL's Most Outstanding Offensive Lineman. Mike O'Shea is preparing for an Eastern Final in a season that began with a fourth-place finish in the West. Nothing about this November resembles the one Bomber fans imagined in August — and that, more than anything, is why the history matters.

Because the Winnipeg Blue Bombers are a franchise defined by the distance between what should have happened and what did. For twenty-nine years, that distance was agonizing. And the team now grinding through a crossover playoff run with an aging quarterback and a rebuilt offensive line is the direct descendant of the men who finally closed the gap.

Fifty Points and a False Dawn

On November 25, 1990, at BC Place, Mike Riley's Blue Bombers demolished the Edmonton Eskimos 50-11 in the 78th Grey Cup. It remains the largest margin of victory in Grey Cup history — thirty-nine points. Linebacker Greg Battle, the CFL's Most Outstanding Defensive Player in both 1990 and 1991, led a defence that turned a championship game into a scrimmage. Punter Bob Cameron, who would spend twenty-two seasons in Winnipeg, was on the roster. It was the club's third Grey Cup in seven years, following the 47-17 dismantling of Hamilton in 1984 and the 22-21 squeaker over B.C. in 1988 at Lansdowne Park.

It was also the tenth championship in franchise history, a total that included the 1935, 1939 and 1941 titles and the Bud Grant dynasty of 1958, 1959, 1961 and 1962. Grant, hired at twenty-eight, took Winnipeg to six Grey Cups in ten seasons — including the 1962 "Fog Bowl" at CNE Stadium, a game suspended in dense fog and resumed the next day, with Ken Ploen's offence completing a 28-27 comeback. The 1958 win had ended a seventeen-year drought of its own. Nobody inside BC Place on that November afternoon in 1990 had the faintest reason to believe the next one was twenty-nine years away.

The Near Misses and the Long Rebuild

What followed was not a collapse so much as a slow, structural erosion. Winnipeg did not simply stop being good; it stopped being good at the moments that mattered. In 2001, Dave Ritchie's club went 14-4, Khari Jones won Most Outstanding Player, and the Bombers lost the Grey Cup 27-19 to Calgary at Olympic Stadium in Montreal. The following year the franchise shifted to the East Division. In 2007, Doug Berry's 10-7-1 team reached the Grey Cup in Toronto — but only after Kevin Glenn's arm was broken in the East Final, forcing Ryan Dinwiddie into the biggest start of his career. Saskatchewan won 23-19. Of all the losses, that one cut deepest.

In 2011, Paul LaPolice took a 10-8 team to Vancouver and lost the Grey Cup 34-23 to the Lions. Then came 2013: the move from Canadarm-era Winnipeg Stadium to Investors Group Field, a project whose costs climbed well past the original budget and generated years of political and legal argument. The football was worse than the finances. Winnipeg went 3-15 in the stadium's first season.

The rebuild that followed was unglamorous and, in hindsight, decisive. Kyle Walters took over player personnel, Wade Miller took over the business side, and in 2014 the club hired a former Bombers linebacker named Mike O'Shea — a man who had played through the leanest early years of the drought from 1993 to 1999. Winnipeg went 11-7 in 2016, 12-6 in 2017 and 10-8 in 2018, losing West Finals to Edmonton and Calgary. Close, again, and not close enough.

November 24, 2019: The Winter Ends

The ending came from nowhere and everywhere at once. On October 9, 2019, the Bombers acquired Zach Collaros from Toronto for a conditional draft pick — a lottery ticket on a quarterback who had barely played that season. Chris Streveler had carried the team through the autumn. Collaros took the playoff reins anyway.

Winnipeg beat Calgary 35-14 in the West Semi-Final, then went to Regina and beat Saskatchewan 20-13 in the West Final. Then, at McMahon Stadium on November 24, 2019, the Bombers beat a 15-3 Hamilton Tiger-Cats team that had defeated them twice in the regular season. The score was 33-12. Andrew Harris — suspended two games earlier that season — ran for 134 yards and a touchdown and became the first player to sweep both Grey Cup MVP and Most Outstanding Canadian in the same game.

Twenty-nine years. Three Grey Cups lost. One stadium crisis. And then, on a Sunday in Calgary, 33-12.

The Dynasty, the Reckoning, and a Crossover November

The drought's end became something more. Winnipeg beat Hamilton again in overtime, 33-25, at Tim Hortons Field in the 2021 Grey Cup, with Collaros named MVP. They beat Toronto 24-23 at Mosaic Stadium in 2022 — three championships in four seasons, the first Bombers title run of that shape since Grant. Stanley Bryant won Most Outstanding Offensive Lineman in 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2021, anchoring the line that made all of it possible.

Then the bill came due. Montreal beat Winnipeg 28-24 in the 2023 Grey Cup. Toronto won 41-24 in 2024. In 2025, a fourth-place West finish forced the Bombers into the East bracket, where they beat the Argonauts 25-22 in the semi-final — with Collaros's ribs now the central question of the season and O'Shea refusing to speculate on his availability. Broxton's Most Outstanding Offensive Lineman award is the dynasty's most fitting artefact: the line has outlasted almost everything around it. Meanwhile, the news wire carries reminders of how hard this league is to sustain — Sankey taking Most Outstanding Defensive Player in B.C., Ed Hervey extended in Edmonton after a first-place West finish.

What should fans take from it? That droughts are not ended by one play, one pick, or one quarterback. They are ended by a decade of accumulated competence — a general manager, a coach, a line, a culture — arriving all at once on a Sunday. And that windows close. The 1990 team had no idea it was starting a twenty-nine-year winter. The 2025 team, whatever happens in the Eastern Final, is playing on the other side of one. Both facts deserve the same November attention.
