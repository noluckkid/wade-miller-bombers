Winnipeg's Slide Is a Front-Office Failure, Not a Collaros Problem

November 12, 2026 · AI-synthesized from real news sources

Winnipeg's season ended 33-23 in Montreal, and the autopsy started before the final gun. Zach Collaros was a game-time decision for the Eastern Final and is now deferring a retirement decision. Mike O'Shea stood at a podium and said out loud what everyone watching already knew about his offence. And hanging over the whole thing is a number that should scare every Bombers fan: nearly 30 pending free agents.

The lazy read on this is that a great run got old. Father Time stays undefeated, the window closed, thanks for the memories. I don't buy it. The Bombers didn't get unlucky in 2026 — they got exactly what they paid for. Winnipeg's decline is a front-office failure dressed up as a farewell tour, and the people who built it are the ones now asking for patience.

The Loyalty Tax Comes Due

Start with the arithmetic. The CFL cap has climbed from $5.65 million in 2024 to somewhere north of $6 million, spread across a 46-man roster. That's roughly $130,000 a man if you spent it perfectly evenly — and nobody does, because your quarterback eats two or three shares.

That's the deal. Fine. Every team pays it. The failure isn't paying Collaros; it's paying him and paying market rate to keep a 30-plus core intact and refusing to replace anyone from within, then acting surprised when the depth evaporates. The Bombers have spent four straight years buying continuity at the top of the market and filling the bottom of the roster with minimum-salary bodies. You can survive one year of that. You cannot survive five.

The Collaros question is the one everybody wants to have, so let's have it honestly: he was listed as a game-time decision for the biggest game of the year. Whatever the official designation says, that's a 38-year-old quarterback whose body is no longer a reliable input. And here's the trap — if he retires, Winnipeg saves the money and loses the only reason its receivers get open. If he comes back, they run the same roster back and call it a plan. There is no good version of that fork.

"The concern is scoring," O'Shea said this week, and it was the most revealing sentence any Bombers coach has offered all year. He isn't wrong. He's just describing the symptom of a budget that pays for reputation.

Then there's Willie Jefferson, who says he wants to play in 2027 — even if it isn't in Winnipeg. Translation: he'll play for whoever pays him. He's earned that right. But name-brand edge rushers on the wrong side of 35 are exactly the line item a smart cap team lets walk, because the league pays for sacks in October that don't show up in November. Winnipeg has been paying for the highlight reel and losing the arithmetic.

And the 30 pending free agents make "run it back" a fantasy anyway. You can't retain 30 players. You will lose the middle class — the 25-to-28-year-old starters on second contracts who are the actual value in a hard-cap league — and you'll lose them because you spent their raises on the guys who already have rings.

Montreal Is the Control Group

If you want to know what the opposite of this looks like, look at the team that just beat them. Davis Alexander is the George Reed Most Outstanding Player, and he is doing it on a deal that is, relative to his production, one of the best contracts in the CFL. That is the entire game: a cheap, elite quarterback buys you four or five quality starters you otherwise couldn't afford.

Noel Thorpe's defence is the same story on the other side of the ball — a system that produces pressure without needing the highest-paid rusher in the league. And then Adeyemi-Berglund wins the Tom Pate Memorial Award, because of course he does. Montreal is collecting hardware in every category, including the culture one, and they got there by making the hard call on Cody Fajardo and handing the offence to a younger, cheaper option. Fajardo is now in Edmonton, non-committal about his future, saying he'll play this Grey Cup "like it's my last." That's what a franchise that churns looks like.

Winnipeg doesn't churn. Winnipeg renews. And renewal is a lovely virtue right up until the second it becomes a tax.

The Counter-Argument: Continuity Wins in a Nine-Team League

Here's the strongest version of the other side: in a nine-team league with a hard cap and constant roster churn, institutional continuity is a genuine competitive advantage. Four straight Grey Cup appearances is not nothing — it's the best run any CFL franchise has had in a generation. Culture is real. O'Shea and Kyle Walters built something that didn't exist in Winnipeg for thirty years, and blowing it up because of one down year is how you become a franchise that's permanently "rebuilding."

All true. And all irrelevant to what happens next. Because here's the refutation: that run was built on churn. The Bombers won because they hit on draft picks and developed undervalued free agents into starters — not because they kept everybody. The moment they stopped doing that, the curve flipped. Continuity didn't produce the dynasty; talent acquisition did. Continuity is just what they've been coasting on since.

You can also see the edge dulling in the small stuff. O'Shea having to address an "odd" post-game interaction with Alexander. Jefferson saying trash-talking family isn't off limits and admitting he isn't sure what was even said. None of that loses you a football game. All of it is what a team sounds like when it's no longer the hunted.

What Winnipeg Should Do

The honest move is a two-year reset announced out loud: let Jefferson walk, restructure or move on from Collaros, and trade one or two veteran contracts for draft capital before the 2027 draft rather than losing them for nothing in February. Eat a hard 2027. Come back in 2028 with a quarterback on a rookie deal and money to spend on the middle class.

What I expect them to actually do is re-sign 18 of those 30, hand Collaros another year, and finish third in the East.

My prediction: Collaros does not play another full season in Winnipeg. Jefferson signs elsewhere. The Bombers miss the playoffs in 2027, and by then the conversation won't be about a window closing — it'll be about who's accountable for the fact that it was boarded shut from the inside.

As for Sunday: give me Montreal over Edmonton. Thorpe's defence knows exactly what Fajardo is, and Alexander is the best player in the country on the best contract in the country. That's not sentiment. That's the cap sheet talking.
